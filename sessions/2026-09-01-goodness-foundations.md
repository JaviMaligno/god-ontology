# Session: Foundations and meanings of goodness

**Date:** 2026-09-01
**Session ID:** S21
**Primary question:** What could divine goodness mean and what could ground it without compromising $G$'s ultimity or emptying goodness into bare choice?
**Dependencies:** C19, C56, C65, C96–C120, C121–C126, S18–S20
**Target depth:** F1 problem architecture and provisional candidate

## Starting position

The inherited authorial preference resists an objective Good construed as a reality ontologically prior to $G$ and takes seriously the proposal that divine choice supplies the standard. The same discussion requires a substantive answer to why $G$'s nature or choices are good and distinguishes what humans believe good from what is actually good for them.

The present pass does not infer that $G$ is good. It separates the target claims, tests unrestricted choice voluntarism, and proposes a relational divine-source account as the working candidate.

## Different goodness questions

| Term | Question | Current status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $GF(x,A,C,\tau)$ | Is $x$ good for subject $A$ under context $C$ and temporal scope $\tau$? After S23 this unindexed-by-dimension form is reserved for a generic or all-things-considered assessment. | open plural account |
| $Excellent(x,K)$ | Does $x$ realize excellences constitutive of its kind or capacities? | open |
| $Valuable(s)$ | Is state or relation $s$ worthy of realization, preservation, or appreciation? | open |
| $Fitting(a,R,C)$ | Does action $a$ appropriately respond to reasons $R$ in context $C$? | provisional evaluative bridge |
| $GoodAgent(A)$ | Does $A$ stably recognize, care about, and respond fittingly to genuine goods and reasons? | not yet established for $G$ |
| $Obligatory(a,A)$ | Is $A$ required to perform $a$? | distinct and open, including whether the same morality governs $G$ and creatures |
| $Omnibenevolent(G)$ | Does $G$ possess and perfectly exercise the relevant good dispositions across their proper scope? | deferred stronger thesis |

Human moral belief is an epistemic state and can fail to track any of these. What is good for a human may depend partly on human nature, consciousness, capacities, relationships, and circumstances rather than on current preference or cultural judgment alone.

## Does objective value threaten ultimacy?

The ultimacy objection is decisive against another independent agent, substance, or efficient source that explains or governs $G$. It does not immediately establish that every logical, modal, mathematical, or normative relation must be a product of divine choice.

Distinguish:

\[
OntologicallyPriorTo(v,G)
\]

from:

\[
NormativelyConstrains(v,a,G).
\]

A fact about fittingness might be structural or relational rather than a separately existing superior being. This parallels $N_L$ and $N_M$: a contradiction is not made coherent by power, yet logic is not thereby another cosmic agent. Whether normative structure can receive the same treatment is open; the distinction only blocks the inference from “objective” to “ontologically prior substance.”

## Four grounding families

### 1. Unrestricted choice voluntarism

\[
Good(x)\quad\text{iff}\quad ChosenAsGood(G,x).
\]

This preserves a strong sense of divine normative sovereignty and matches the author's initial preference. It faces three internal pressures:

1. **evaluative emptiness:** “$G$ is good” risks reducing to “$G$ chooses what $G$ chooses”;
2. **reason-order problem:** if value is created only by the completed choice, value cannot supply a reason guiding that choice in grounding order;
3. **arbitrary reversal:** the account needs a principled answer to whether any coherent cruelty, betrayal, or destruction would become good solely through divine endorsement.

The theory remains logically describable, especially if goodness is explicitly defined as divine endorsement. It is currently disfavored as a substantive explanation of divine goodness rather than rejected as incoherent.

Its strongest reply to the reason-order pressure is that $G$ chooses from non-evaluative motives or basic preferences and the choice then constitutes value. That preserves logical coherence and genuine agency. The cost is explanatory: the motives cannot themselves show that the choice is good, and “divine goodness” remains self-certifying unless some additional evaluative structure is admitted.

#### Voluntarism is not one thesis

The current objection targets **unrestricted axiological voluntarism**. It leaves several separable claims open:

| Form | Role of divine will or approval |
| --- | --- |
| Semantic | “good” means approved or chosen by $G$ |
| Axiological/metaphysical | approval or choice constitutes goodness |
| Deontic/command | commands constitute obligations |
| Epistemic | approval reveals or reliably tracks an independently grounded good |
| Institutional | divine acts establish contingent roles, commitments, permissions, or obligations |

The original proposal “good is whatever $G$ approves” is ambiguous between the first two. It remains a live option, but it must specify whether it is defining language, grounding a property, or both.

### 2. Divine-nature standard

Goodness may be identified with resemblance or conformity to $G$'s nature. This stabilizes value beyond token choice but cannot begin with the unexplained premise that the nature is good. The project must identify good-making features or show why divine nature is the ultimate non-arbitrary standard. Full simplicity and perfection are unavailable as shortcuts.

### 3. Independent normative realism

Normative truths may obtain independently of divine will. This gives content to reasons and divine goodness, but its ontology and epistemology require explanation. A Platonic realm of value would add fundamental reality; a structural or relational realism might be lighter and need not compete causally with $G$.

### 4. Relational divine-source account

The working candidate distributes explanatory roles:

- $G$ existentially grounds all actual created bearers of finite value, their natures, capacities, and relational possibilities;
- facts about what benefits or perfects a created subject arise from the fit among conscious life, capacities, sourcehood, development, relationships, and context;
- divine reason and nature can supply stable recognition and concern for such goods, if independently supported;
- divine choice actualizes particular goods and may institute contingent ends, roles, promises, covenants, or obligations without making every evaluative truth true by bare fiat.

Schematically:

\[
GroundsExistence(G,A,C)
\land Fits_d(x,Profile(A,C),\tau)
\Rightarrow CandidatePTGF_d(x,A,C,\tau).
\]

The formula is a research schema, not a reductive definition. $Profile(A,C)$ must not be limited to occurrent desire: an agent can misunderstand or damage its own good. Candidate constituents include conscious flourishing, agency, truth, understanding, relationship, embodied capacities, and meaningful development. Their exact list and weighting remain open.

This account makes $G$ the existential source of actual finite goods without making divine endorsement the sole good-making property. It still owes an ultimate account of normative fit and of the evaluative content of $G$'s own nature.

### What “emerges from G” must mean

The author's preferred direction treats normativity as emerging from $G$ and connecting $G$ to the universe. The word “emerges” is not yet an explanation. At least four typed roles must be distinguished:

1. **Existential grounding:** $G$ makes actual the subjects, capacities, relations, and situations that bear finite value.
2. **Value constitution:** features of $G$, conscious subjects, or their relation make something good, bad, fitting, or unfitting.
3. **Recognition and approval:** $G$ apprehends and favors a value without that attitude necessarily constituting it.
4. **Institution:** a divine act creates a contingent role, end, commitment, permission, or obligation.

A future normative bridge can combine them:

\[
NormBridge_G(A,C)=
\langle
GroundExist_G,
ConstituteValue,
Recognize_G,
Institute_G
\rangle.
\]

The tuple is a typing discipline, not yet a theory. The next pass must say which component grounds which evaluative truth and how the components depend on $G$'s necessary nature, free will, and the actual or possible profile of the created subject.

## What the present ontology already contributes

The existing architecture provides possible evidence, not a completed goodness proof:

- S18 supplies reasons favoring finite otherness and analogous sourcehood;
- S19 derives a defeasible concern not to replace that sourcehood unnecessarily;
- S20 distinguishes making personal relation available from forcing reciprocity;
- C108–C111 deny that rational divine choice requires a unique optimum, a best possible world, or necessary creation.

If sourcehood, flourishing, truth, and relationship are later vindicated as goods, these patterns support a stable divine orientation toward them. At present, calling the patterns morally good would assume the evaluative theory being built.

## Freedom, optimality, and requirement

Goodness need not collapse divine agency:

\[
Fitting(G,a,R)
\not\Rightarrow
Required(G,a,R).
\]

Reasons can justify or favor an act without requiring it. Several worlds may realize incomparable or differently balanced goods, and non-creation can remain possible. Neither an optimal world nor necessary creation is included in the minimal goodness target.

## Adversarial tests

| Objection | Result |
| --- | --- |
| Any objective good is a being above $G$ | Rejected as an inference; normative relation, existential ground, and superior agent are different categories |
| Divine choice alone gives the cleanest ultimate stopping point | Live but explanatorily thin; it makes divine goodness and pre-choice evaluative reasons difficult to state substantively |
| Divine nature solves arbitrariness automatically | Rejected; without good-making content it relocates the question |
| Creaturely flourishing reduces goodness to human preference | Rejected by the candidate account; preference is evidence and a constituent in some cases, not the sole good-maker |
| A relational theory makes value merely relative and therefore unreal | Not established; relational properties can be objective given the relata and conditions |
| A good God must create the unique best world | Rejected at the current level; plural, incomparable, or merely justifying reasons remain possible |
| The same moral obligations must govern $G$ and created agents | Open; differences in dependence, power, roles, and vulnerability may change the applicable norms |

## Decisions

| Decision | Status | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Separate good-for, excellence, value, fitting action, good agency, obligation, and omnibenevolence | accepted | Prevents one undefined use of “good” from doing every job |
| Infer goodness from necessity, agency, power, or creatorhood | rejected | None supplies evaluative content by itself |
| Treat unrestricted divine-choice voluntarism as the current solution | open/disfavored | Preserves ultimity but faces emptiness, reason-order, and arbitrary-reversal pressures |
| Treat divine nature as good by definition | rejected | This would rename rather than answer the grounding question |
| Explore the relational divine-source account | provisional candidate | Preserves divine existential ultimity while giving created goods non-arbitrary relational content |
| Require an optimal world or necessary creation | rejected | Good reasons can be plural, incomparable, and non-requiring |
| Attribute full goodness or omnibenevolence to $G$ now | not yet earned | The value ground, divine evaluative disposition, and proper scope remain open |

## Literature and novelty notes

- The [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on theological voluntarism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/voluntarism-theological/) explains why voluntarist accounts often restrict themselves to a subset of morality: some independent evaluative content appears necessary to explain divine goodness and the intelligibility of divine commands.
- [Robert Merrihew Adams, *Finite and Infinite Goods*](https://doi.org/10.1093/0195153715.001.0001) makes God the archetype of excellence while treating obligation through divine command. This is a precedent for distributing good and obligation rather than reducing every evaluative property to token choice.
- [Mark C. Murphy, *God's Own Ethics*](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780198796916.001.0001) challenges the assumption that divine and human moral agency fall under one undifferentiated norm and uses the distinction between justifying and requiring reasons.
- The [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on well-being](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/well-being/) distinguishes what is good for a subject from moral rightness and surveys hedonist, desire, and objective-list accounts.

The proposed relational divine-source package is not claimed as novel. Its integration with this project's typed grounding, non-necessitating reasons, conscious sourcehood, providence, and personal availability is currently an unassessed synthesis.
