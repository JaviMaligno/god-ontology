# Session: The accessible track and the experiential register

**Date:** 2026-09-02
**Session ID:** S34
**Primary question:** How should the project express its beliefs — in God, in the kind of God, in the good and its objectivity — in ordinary language derived from the ontology, and how should it relate the argumentative approach to the experiential one without dismissing either?
**Dependencies:** research method (four evidential layers), C121–C126, C183–C184, challenge register Q0
**Starting ledger items:** primacy of interaction, constructive comparative standard, C02, C122–C124
**Target depth:** F1 for experiential access; methodological decisions for the accessible track

## Authorial direction

The author opens a process parallel to the hard metaphysics. Its purpose is to be able to say, to someone with no metaphysical background and no patience for the formality of the ontology, why the author believes in God, what kind of God, why there is such a thing as the good, and why that good is objective. The answers must come from the metaphysics, but in a language the listener can follow.

The author's own example answer to “Why do you believe in God?” has three moves: it is unsatisfying, and contrary to the spirit of scientific curiosity, to stop at a universe that exists because it exists; beyond that stop there are two families, a mathematical structure from which everything follows and a conscious agent who chooses; and the agent is preferred because it does not give determinism and connects with what we are as conscious agents. S35 gives those moves a formal backbone.

The author also wants the process to reach personal and human experience. Using the popular image of two hemispheres — a left one that is rational, logical, and analytic, and a right one that is creative and hosts subjective experience — the ontological essay is very much a left-hemisphere work, while approaching God through art, music, and religious experience belongs to the right. Neither activity is confined to one hemisphere, and neither approach should be dismissed. The formal work must therefore be translated, and it must connect with the experiential approach.

Two later additions in the same brief widen the process: the accessible track should drive the filling of gaps in the metaphysics, under the author's criteria and along the author's line, so that plain answers have a rigorous background; and there should be a bank of reasonable questions, similar to the challenge register, answered from the accessible approach.

## Design decisions

### Two registers, one ontology

The project now maintains two registers of exposition over one set of commitments:

| Register | Function | Canonical location | What it may claim |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| argumentative | build, test, and record the ontology | `ontology/`, `sessions/`, treatise | whatever the ledger supports, at the ledger's status |
| accessible | express the same commitments in ordinary language, in the first person, and connect them with human experience | `writing/accessible/` | nothing beyond the ledger; the status of each claim in plain words |

The accessible register is downstream: it cannot change a commitment. It is not passive: when a plain answer would need more than the ledger supports, the shortfall is a research item that enters the active queue under the priority discipline (authorial direction ranks first). S35 and S36 are the first two items produced this way.

### The hemisphere image is a metaphor

Hemispheric lateralization is real for some functions, but the popular “rational left, creative right” division is not an established neuroscientific description of reasoning and creativity. Under C183–C184 the project cannot use it as an empirical premise. It keeps the image as a metaphor for two registers of approach, argument and experience, and says so wherever the image appears. Iain McGilchrist's *The Master and His Emissary* is the nearest cultural reference and is recorded as a research pointer, not as a source of premises.

### The fourth evidential layer

The research method already lists experiential and theological context as a fourth evidential layer that establishes its own warrant and limits and is not dismissed because deductive metaphysics cannot reach it. The accessible track is where that layer is actually used. The session therefore asks what the ontology itself says about experiential access to $G$.

## Experiential access in the ontology

### What is available

From S20, $G$ is universally personally available (C122): $G$ can recognize, receive an address from, address, and respond directly to any actual conscious subject. Availability is a capacity; exercise is distinct (C123); reciprocal relation is asymmetrically co-produced and may involve the creature's libertarian participation (C124).

Define a divine direct address:

\[
Address(G,A,t)
\]

as an exercise of regard or response directed at subject $A$ at creaturely time $t$. In the intervention taxonomy this is a circumstantial or informational contribution to $A$'s deliberative situation (C41), or a bare exercise of regard with no further causal target. It is never a source override, on pain of destroying the relation it was meant to make possible; the agency-preservation presumption (C115) applies to it.

### Three things an experience can be

Let $Exp_G(A,e,t)$ mean that $A$ has an experience $e$ whose content presents $G$ as present, addressing, or responding. Given ontological omnipresence (C91), three cases must be separated:

| Case | Truthmaker | Comment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| veridical of standing presence | $Present_O(G,A)$, which holds for every actual subject | the experience discloses something always true; it requires no token intervention |
| veridical of exercised regard | $Address(G,A,t)$ or a response | a token exercise of availability; a lower-control intervention or a bare regard |
| non-veridical | neither, although $Present_O$ still holds | the phenomenal content misattributes; the ontology does not say this never happens |

The first row is the ontology's most distinctive contribution to the experiential question: an experience of “being in the presence of God” can be true of a standing fact, because in this model there is no place where $G$ is not grounding, knowing, and available. The second row is where prayer, response, and vocation would be located if actual. The third row is the reason the register cannot be self-authenticating.

### Epistemic status

Under C02 the project takes seriously that scientific method does not exhaust every route to knowledge, while requiring any further route to establish its warrant. The session records:

1. $Exp_G$ is defeasible first-person evidence of presence or relation for the subject who has it, subject to ordinary defeaters (known naturalistic explanation of the token, incoherence with established truths, unreliability of the practice).
2. $Exp_G$ is not evidence for the specific architecture: no experience can establish ACO, E+, or the intervention taxonomy. The architecture is the argumentative register's task.
3. The two registers meet at C122. The argument establishes that relation is available; it cannot exercise the relation. Experience is participation in a relation that, by C124, requires the creature's contribution. Therefore argument and experience are complementary, not competing: one says that the door exists and is open, the other walks through it.
4. Absence of $Exp_G$ refutes nothing in the model (C123: capacity is not exercise; C124: participation is co-produced). Presence of $Exp_G$ does not entail a miracle (C17) or any intervention beyond regard.

### Art, music, and the anchors

Aesthetic and religious experience engage two anchors directly: conscious valence ($P$) and shared attention on something not made by the receiver ($R$, in the S20 sense of joint attention). In this ontology every actual intelligible structure is grounded in $G$ (C73, C91), so attentive apprehension of order, beauty, or meaning is apprehension of something whose ground is $G$. Whether such apprehension is *itself* a mediated relation to $G$, rather than a relation to created structure that $G$ grounds, is left open. The accessible track may say that art and music are a real way of attending to what the argument can only point at, and must not say that they prove anything about the argument.

## Adversarial tests

| Objection | Result |
| --- | --- |
| Experience is private and unfalsifiable, so it has no place in the project | It has a place in the fourth evidential layer with stated limits; it is not admitted as evidence for the architecture |
| If God is always present, every feeling of presence is trivially veridical | Only the standing-presence case is; the exercised-regard case has a separate truthmaker and can fail; misattribution remains possible |
| The hemisphere framing is pseudoscience | Treated as a metaphor and excluded as a premise (R74) |
| Two registers will drift apart | The accessible answers carry ledger provenance and are revised whenever a cited commitment changes |
| An accessible “I believe” hides the ledger's status | The method requires status words in plain language: what has been established, what is preferred, what is believed beyond the result, what is not known |

## Literature and novelty notes

Cited from memory; not verified online in this session.

- William Alston, *Perceiving God* (Cornell, 1991), treats religious experience as a doxastic practice with defeaters; Richard Swinburne's principle of credulity (*The Existence of God*, 2nd ed., 2004) treats it as defeasible evidence. C292 belongs to that family and is weaker: it limits the evidence to presence and relation.
- William James, *The Varieties of Religious Experience* (1902), and Rudolf Otto, *Das Heilige* (1917), describe the phenomenology; Eleonore Stump's account of second-person presence and shared attention (*Wandering in Darkness*, 2010) is already the precedent for S20.
- The Stanford Encyclopedia entry on religious experience surveys the evidential debate.
- Iain McGilchrist, *The Master and His Emissary* (Yale, 2009), for the hemisphere image as a cultural reference only.

Classification: established components; the project-specific element is the three-way veridicality distinction derived from ontological omnipresence and personal availability, which is a consequence of S15 and S20 rather than a new theory of religious experience.
