# Session: Permission, covers, and the P-gap

**Date:** 2026-09-02
**Session ID:** S38
**Primary question:** Under what conditions could a $G$ whose established profile is good-oriented toward sourcehood and relation permit created suffering without misfit toward conscious valence, and what exactly remains open?

## Starting position and authorial line

S37 leaves $GoodOrient_P(G)$ open. The inherited authorial line supplies three constraints that this session keeps separate:

1. [U16](../corpus/transcript.md#user--u16): the reason evil exists is that, in order for autonomous agents to exist, there are other consequences in the world, particularly those we call evil. This is the structural line and is the primary material.
2. [U16](../corpus/transcript.md#user--u16): struggle may be needed for people to become better, as spoiling children does not. The author explicitly marks this as an extra, more theological and people-grounded layer that must not be mixed with the argument. It is therefore recorded as optional and not used in the derivation.
3. [U11](../corpus/transcript.md#user--u11): we simplify too much by assuming that everything humans perceive as bad is bad from $G$'s perspective; it may serve a greater purpose or be good for another being. R06 records this. It is used here as a fallibility caveat (C168), not as a blanket dissolution.

The question in this architecture is not the classical one. Full goodness and unrestricted power are unearned, so the inference from evil to non-existence does not run. The question is whether the good-oriented profile can be extended to $P$ at all, and what that extension would have to contain.

## Definitions

| Term/symbol | Definition used in this session |
| --- | --- |
| $s$ | A token of grave $P$-misfit for bearer $B$: experienced damage, loss of due conscious integrity, or grave involuntary suffering (S28) |
| $Orig(G,s)$ | $G$ directly originates $s$ through an intervention |
| $Arena(G,s)$ | $G$ existentially grounds the agents, structure, and conditions in which $s$ occurs (C32) |
| $Permits(G,s)$ | $Occurs(s)\land CanPrevent(G,s)\land\neg Prevents(G,s)$ |
| $CanPrevent(G,s)$ | Some available intervention, including lower-control circumstantial ones, would have prevented $s$ (C41, C74, C122) |
| $Cover(r,s)$ | Reason $r$ covers the permission of $s$: preventing $s$ by the available means would produce misfit toward some anchor $a'$, bearer $B'$, scope, or horizon that is undefeated relative to $s$'s misfit |
| $Gratuitous(s)$ | $Permits(G,s)\land\neg\exists r\,Cover(r,s)$ |
| policy-level cover | A cover that justifies a general pattern of non-prevention rather than the permission of this token for this bearer |
| bearer-level cover | A cover located in $B$'s own horizon: fit for $B$ that integrates, compensates, or defeats $s$ within $B$'s extended life |

## Step 1: which divine relation bears the weight

Under C32 and C191, $G$ is not the contrastive author of a creature's moral evil, and under C114 natural suffering generated by a reason-supported $S$ receives derivative explanation without separate moral justification. Neither relation is where the P-gap sits. The load-bearing relation is **permission**: given qualified power and universal availability, $G$ could have prevented $s$, at least by a circumstantial intervention, and did not.

\[
GoodOrient_P(G)\;\Rightarrow\;\neg\exists s\in H^*\,Gratuitous(s).
\]

So the P-gap reduces to the question whether every actual permitted suffering is covered. The session's task is to say what covers are available from the existing ontology, at which level they operate, and what they leave.

## Step 2: covers derived from existing commitments

### Source cover (moral suffering)

For $s$ originated by a created agent $A$, prevention has three routes (C39, C41): source override, option closure, or circumstantial intervention. The first two remove $S$ for that token; C115 gives a defeasible presumption against them. Circumstantial prevention is lower-control and remains available for tokens.

The cover is therefore not that $G$ cannot prevent moral harm. It is policy-level: a policy of preventing every harmful outcome of created choice, by whatever route, would make choices without consequences. That conflicts with the central creation reason (C106: created sourcehood must contribute contrastive content) and with agency ecology (C118: development, salience, and long-run authorship require that acts matter). It also conflicts with $R$: a world in which harmful choices never harm systematically misrepresents what choices are.

\[
SystematicPrevent(G,\text{moral }s)\Rightarrow Misfit_*(\cdot,S,\mathbf A,C,\tau)\land Misfit_*(\cdot,R,\mathbf A,C,\tau).
\]

This is the formal version of the U16 structural line. It leaves a known residue: a good-oriented $G$ could prevent some tokens, especially the gravest, without collapsing the policy. The reply that any threshold is arbitrary, because the worst permitted case just below it raises the same question, is recorded with its precedent and its contested status; the project does not treat it as settled.

### Regularity cover (natural suffering)

C107 states why non-agentic domains exist: shared regularity, information, consequences, embodiment, and secondary causation. Reason-guided sourcehood needs a world whose structure is reliable enough to act in, and truthful relation needs a world that does not mask itself. A policy of constant preventive intervention against natural suffering would erode both:

\[
ConstantPrevent(G,\text{natural }s)\Rightarrow\neg Regular(S)\Rightarrow Misfit_*(\cdot,S,\mathbf A,C,\tau)\land Misfit_*(\cdot,R,\mathbf A,C,\tau).
\]

Again the cover is policy-level and again it leaves the threshold residue.

### Developmental cover (optional, unused)

The thesis that robust agency requires difficulty would supply a further cover for some suffering. Following the author's own classification in U16, it remains an optional theological layer; it is not used in the derivation, and the novelty map continues to list it as unassessed and high-burden.

### Perspective caveat

C168 makes finite judgment of fit fallible in both directions. R06 forbids assuming that every perceived evil is evil from the complete perspective; C184-style honesty forbids using that as a blanket. Apparent gratuitousness is defeasible evidence against $GoodOrient_P$, not proof, and its apparent absence is not proof either.

## Step 3: the bearer-level remainder

Both structural covers are transpersonal or ecological. They explain why the *policy* of permission is fit. $Fit_*$ is bearer-indexed, and generic good is undefeated fit across bearers and horizons (S27). A policy-level cover can therefore leave bearer-level misfit undefeated for $B$:

\[
PolicyCover(s)\not\Rightarrow\neg Misfit_*(s,P,B,C,\tau_B).
\]

The $R$ anchor blocks the shortcut of treating $B$'s suffering as a mere vehicle for others' goods: truthful recognition of $B$ as a subject is itself an anchor, and a permission fit only as a means to $\mathbf A$'s agency is a misfit toward $B$ in $R$. So bearer-level closure requires a cover within $B$'s own horizon $\tau_B$.

Under E+ later goods for $B$ are as actual as earlier ones, and C149 already allows diachronic integration to defeat local misfit. For many sufferers this is available in life. For a bearer whose life ends in uncovered suffering — the paradigm hard cases — the only possible bearer-level cover lies beyond death. Hence:

\[
\neg Gratuitous(s)\ \text{for such }B
\;\Rightarrow\;
\exists\tau_B\supsetneq Life(B)\,\big[Fit_*(x,P,B,C,\tau_B)\ \text{undefeated}\big].
\]

This is the session's structural result. In this ontology the problem of evil does not push toward denying $G$ or toward skeptical theism; it pushes **personal continuation** onto the trunk. Whether $B$ has an extended horizon is now a load-bearing open question (CH17). The hell-as-privation model of S28 already presupposes post-mortem subjects provisionally; the present result gives that presupposition a second and stronger dependency.

## Step 4: what a positive $P$-orientation would add

The covers above are permissions. A positive divine disposition toward $P$ would be exercised regard toward sufferers: address, accompaniment, consolation, response (C121–C123, C303). The ontology establishes the capacity and not the exercise. If $G$ has valence, co-suffering would be a further $P$-relation; divine valence remains open (S37). These are recorded as what would extend $GoodOrient_P$ positively, not as results.

## Step 5: the exact content of the remaining belief

The accessible answer to “Is God good, also in what we feel?” now has precise content. To believe it is to believe:

\[
\neg\exists s\in H^*\,Gratuitous(s)
\quad\text{and}\quad
\forall B\,[\text{uncovered }s\text{ for }B\Rightarrow\exists\tau_B\supsetneq Life(B)\ \text{with undefeated }P\text{-fit}].
\]

This belief is held under the S28 epistemic-orientation profile (C200–C202): belief, trust, or hope directed at an undefeated anchor, with non-culpable attention to counterevidence. The counterevidence is exactly the set of apparently gratuitous cases. This is what “faith” means in the accessible register: not belief without evidence, but practical orientation under incomplete evidence, with its content and its defeaters stated.

## Literature and novelty notes

Cited from memory; not verified online in this session.

- Richard Swinburne, *Providence and the Problem of Evil* (Oxford, 1998): natural-law and free-will theodicies; the regularity cover belongs to that family.
- John Hick, *Evil and the God of Love* (1966): soul-making; corresponds to the optional developmental layer.
- Peter van Inwagen, *The Problem of Evil* (Oxford, 2006): the no-minimum or arbitrary-threshold reply, recorded here as contested.
- William Rowe, “The Problem of Evil and Some Varieties of Atheism” (1979): the evidential argument from apparently gratuitous suffering; Stephen Wykstra and Michael Bergmann on skeptical theism. R83–R84 reject both blanket moves as inferences.
- Marilyn McCord Adams, *Horrendous Evils and the Goodness of God* (Cornell, 1999): argues that a good God must defeat horrendous evils within the sufferer's own life, including post-mortem. This is the closest precedent for C314–C315; the project reaches the bearer-level requirement from the bearer-indexed $Fit_*$ and the $R$ anchor rather than from a perfection premise.
- Eleonore Stump, *Wandering in Darkness* (Oxford, 2010): second-person accounts of suffering and union; relevant to Step 4.

Classification: established families; the project-specific element is the derivation of the bearer-level remainder from bearer-indexed fit and the $R$ anchor, and the resulting promotion of personal continuation to a trunk dependency without a perfection or omnibenevolence premise.
