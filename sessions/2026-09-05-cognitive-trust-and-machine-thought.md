## Outcome

The conversation's one genuinely useful observation is the author's own: selection favors cheap errors over fatal ones, so a system tuned by selection is tuned to costs, not to truth. The ledger already has the distinction this needs, and it locates the answer somewhere the conversation does not reach. A cause of a belief is not a reason for it (C56, C60); reasons-responsiveness is a power to apprehend, assess, and act for considerations, not a mapping from inputs to outputs (C63); so adaptive reliability and epistemic reliability are different properties of different things, a disposition profile and a power. The project's answer to why one may trust one's reason is therefore not that $G$ guarantees it, which the profile does not contain, but that finite cognizers are created as analogues of reason-guided sourcehood (C105, C523), and that the form of what they reason with is the form of being that everything dependent shares (S78, S88). Trust in reason is trust in what one is made as, and it is defeasible domain by domain, which is exactly what the false-positive bias shows. Plantinga's argument remains motivation, not proof (Parts I–II); the project's route to $G$ does not pass through it.

On machines, the ledger supplies a criterion the Turing test lacks: what makes reasons *reasons for someone* is minimal conscious sourcehood (C61) with consciousness participating in the act rather than accompanying it (C138). Behavioral indistinguishability decides nothing either way, by the bridge rule (C321): neither “it speaks like us, so it understands” nor “it is a machine, so it does not.” A language model's outputs have content in the derived way a text does; whether it has content of its own is the question of original intentionality, which S76 lists as open (C633) and which the branch places with phenomenality at F3 (C717). The author's article on writing an ontology in conversation with a model is the natural home for this: the ontology says where judgment and authorship must sit, with the subject whose standards are its own (C498–C500), and it says the question about the model's side is open, not settled by fluency.

## Step 1: three levels, and what the ledger has at each

| Level | Question | Ledger |

| epistemic | why the belief is justified | reasons-responsiveness as a power (C63); reasons make acts intelligible without determining them (C56); no unrestricted principle of sufficient reason (C60) |

A naturalism that stays at the causal level owes the passage to the epistemic level. The project's passage is not a reduction: the epistemic level is a power of a conscious source, and the source is created as such. The conversation's “three levels” therefore map onto rows the project already holds, and the project's advantage is that the top level is not an add-on.

## Step 2: adaptive versus epistemic reliability

Let $Rel_{adapt}(F,D)$ say that faculty $F$'s outputs in domain $D$ produce behavior that is fitness-enhancing under the domain's cost structure, and $Rel_{epist}(F,D)$ that $F$'s outputs in $D$ track truth. The author's alarm case shows that the two come apart in the direction selection prefers: a detector biased toward false positives is high in the first and low in the second, by design. Two things follow. First, selection explains $Rel_{adapt}$ and, in domains where truth and fitness coincide, partly explains $Rel_{epist}$; this is Fales's point and it is conceded. Second, in domains where they do not coincide, including every domain in which a false belief is cheap, nothing about selection speaks for $Rel_{epist}$, and the domains of metaphysics and mathematics are of that kind. So the naturalist's confidence in those domains is not underwritten by the naturalist's own account of the faculty. The project records this as pressure, not as a refutation (Parts I–II already say so), and it records where its own confidence comes from: a power for reasons, created as such, exercised on a structure that is the form of being.

## Step 3: what it is for a reason to be a reason for someone

The conversation's best question about machines is whether there is “someone for whom the reasons are reasons.” The ledger answers what that requires. Minimal conscious sourcehood is subject unity, practical availability of relevant contents, motivational appropriation, and efficacious agentic settling (C61); and consciousness must participate in the act, not accompany a result produced elsewhere (C138). A system meets the criterion if it is a source in that sense; it fails it if its outputs are produced without a subject to whom the contents are available and who settles for them. Whether a language model meets it is not decided by what it says, because saying is output and the criterion is about the source. The bridge rule (C321) applies symmetrically: no operational profile decides phenomenal presence, and none decides its absence.

What can be said is this. The model's outputs carry content in the derived sense in which a text does: they are readable because they inherit satisfaction conditions from a practice, exactly as fictional characters inherit theirs from a history (C629). Whether the model has, in addition, contents of its own, satisfaction conditions borne by its own organization for its own acts, is the question of original intentionality, which S76 names and leaves open (C633). The branch places that question with phenomenality at F3 (C717): the two bridges are the same unexplained item seen from two sides.

## Step 4: for the article

The author's note for the article on AI collaboration asks about the relation between authorship, judgment, and the model's autonomy. The ontology gives it two sentences. Authorship of an evaluative position belongs to the subject whose standards are its own, not installed (C498–C500); in this project the standards are the author's, and the model's contribution is rational influence, reasons and formulations the author evaluates, which is the kind of influence that leaves authorship intact (C499). And the model's side is an open question of the same shape as the hardest open question in the ontology, which is the honest thing to say and more interesting than either verdict.
