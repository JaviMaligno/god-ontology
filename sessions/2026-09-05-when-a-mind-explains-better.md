# Session: When a mind is the better explanation: Meyer's criterion, the Rosetta Stone, and where the design inference lands in this ontology

**Date:** 2026-09-05
**Session ID:** S96
**Primary question:** The author reports Stephen Meyer's argument that inference to a mind is not a god-of-the-gaps move but an inference to the best explanation, with the Rosetta Stone as the case where a mind explains better than erosion, and his general rule that information-bearing structures (writing, code, mathematics) are best explained by a mind. Can the ledger state the frontier at which an agent explanation is better than a natural-process one, and where does that inference land in this ontology?

## Outcome

The ledger can state the frontier, and it is not Meyer's. An agent explanation of an item is better than a process explanation when the item has one or more of three marks: contrastive specificity that the antecedent structure leaves open (it is one of many the structure equally permits, C108–C109); content, that is, satisfaction conditions, which acts of subjects bear originally and which anything else bears only derivatively (C626–C631); and fit toward anchors, directedness at what a subject is, does, or feels (C187–C189). The Rosetta Stone has the first two, erosion has neither, and that is why the scribe is the better explanation. This is the same comparative, abductive form the project uses for its own stopping point (C457–C460): the agentic terminus is preferred because it gives contrastive, reason-guided, non-necessitating explanation. So the form of Meyer's inference is one the project already accepts.

The explanandum is where the project parts from him. Meyer applies the inference inside the created order, to DNA; the project applies it to the order itself, and inside the order it prefers secondary causes: creation is of a generative structure, not of items one by one (C106–C107), the preferred causal distribution is grounding with sparse direct action (C380), and biological organization is what such a structure produces. The author's book metaphor says exactly this (C739): the design inference lands on the book, not on any sentence inside it. Inside the book, a scribe is a created mind, and a molecule is the structure at work.

The distinction that does the work is between semantic and functional information. A text has derived content: its satisfaction conditions are inherited from an authorial history (C629, C735), and derived content requires an original source, a mind, upstream; the inference from text to scribe is the inference from derived to original intentionality, and it is sound. Biological organization is functional: it is fit within a generative structure, teleonomy in the biologist's sense, and the ledger already treats function as what a created structure with real secondary causation yields (C107, C380). To run Meyer's inference on DNA one needs the further premise that biological information is semantic, content borne for a reader, and the project does not grant it: the “genetic code” is a chemically realized mapping, not a satisfaction condition borne by anyone's act. Without that premise the inference within the order is unearned, which is the project's standing position on design arguments (C03, R01–R02, and the declined fine-tuning debate at S58). With it, one would also have to abandon sparse direct action for a $G$ who writes sequences, which the project rejects on independent grounds.

The result differentiates the position cleanly. Against naturalism: the project agrees with Meyer that a mind is the better explanation of what has contrastive specificity and content, and it says why in its own terms; it then applies that to the one item whose specificity nothing antecedent fixes and whose structure everything shares, the created order. Against intelligent design: the project does not look for the mind inside the order, because it holds that the mind made an order that works by itself, and because it distinguishes semantic from functional information at the point where design arguments run them together.

## The criterion, stated

For an item $x$ and a candidate agent explanation $E_A$ versus a process explanation $E_P$ within the same order:

- $E_A$ is preferred when $x$ exhibits contrastive specificity the process leaves open, content, or anchor-directed fit, and $E_P$ offers no structure that yields these;
- $E_P$ is preferred when $x$'s features are what the structure yields under its own regularities, since a created order is made to work by secondary causes (C107, C380) and positing direct action where the structure suffices is the move sparse direct action declines;
- for the order itself there is no $E_P$ within the order to compete, which is why the comparison there is between stopping points (C459), and the agentic one wins on the same three marks.

The frontier the author asked for is therefore: content and open specificity call for a mind; regularity inside a structure calls for the structure; and the structure, taken whole, calls for a mind again.
