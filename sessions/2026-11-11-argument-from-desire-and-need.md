# Session: The argument from desire and the human need for God

**Date:** 2026-11-11
**Session ID:** S132
**Primary question:** Does the anchoring of natural desire and need in real objects support the reality of the good, of God and of life beyond death?
**Dependencies:** C105, C123–C124, C190, C457, C466–C471, C954, C959; CH20.
**Depth:** F1 formulation with inspected sources. No formalization trigger.

## Author input

What we desire is based on something that exists: we hunger and there is food, we thirst and there is water. The relation is not causal; everything we desire is anchored in something real. We also desire the good, God and eternal life, so these should be anchored in something real. The author has heard the point taken beyond desire to need and wants to explore the need for God in the human being.

## Definitions

| Term | Definition used in this session |
| --- | --- |
| natural desire | A desire that arises from what we are: universal, unchosen, and such that lasting lack of its object damages us |
| acquired desire | A desire composed by imagination or culture out of real elements; it varies from person to person |
| need | A natural desire whose object is required for the nature to be whole, shown by what its privation does |
| anchoring | Correspondence between a natural desire and a real kind of object able to satisfy it; not a causal claim and not a guarantee of individual satisfaction |

## Argument

### Desire

1. Every natural desire corresponds to something real that can satisfy it: food, water, rest, company, knowledge.
2. Human beings have a natural desire that nothing in this world satisfies: for a good without limit, complete truth, unending life, and God.
3. Therefore, most probably, something beyond this world exists that can satisfy it.

The natural–acquired distinction carries the first premise. A wish to fly or to own a particular thing is assembled from real pieces and is not universal; it is no counterexample. The inference is one of best explanation, in the same family as the author's signs of a mind (C954): hunger indicates food as a law indicates a legislator.

### Need

Need is the stronger term. A whim can go unmet without loss; an unmet need damages. Human beings deprived of meaning, of a final good and of hope are damaged even when every other want is supplied, and no finite good ends the search. A universal need with nothing able to answer it would be unique in nature. The project's account of evil as privation of a due good (C190) describes the same structure: a lack is felt as a lack because something is due.

### Convergence

- **From above.** If a conscious agent created finite persons as analogues of itself and for relation (C105, C124), such a need is to be expected. Without such an agent it stands unexplained.
- **With S131.** Aquinas identifies a natural desire to know the cause of what we observe, which does not rest until it reaches the first cause. This is the explanatory demand of C457 seen as a desire. If natural desires are anchored, the demand for an ultimate explanation is anchored too.

### Scope

Hunger shows that food exists, not that this person will eat. The argument supports the reality of what is sought, not each person's attainment of it. It belongs to the experiential layer (C466–C471) and adds to the cumulative case; it does not replace the contingency and moral routes.

## Adversarial tests

| Objection | Present result |
| --- | --- |
| We desire impossible things: to fly, never to age | These are acquired or composite desires; the premise concerns natural ones. |
| The first premise cannot be known without checking the disputed case | It is a generalization from every other natural desire together with what a natural desire is. Hence the conclusion is probable, not demonstrated. |
| Not everyone feels this desire | Everyone seeks a happiness that no finite good completes; many name its object differently. Connects with P35. |
| Selection can produce a desire with no object | For biological desires selection requires the object: hunger without food would not persist. A universal desire with no object would have to be a by-product, the same move found wanting for morality in S130 (C944, C953). |
| The longing is an emotion, not a desire, or the analogy with hunger is weak | Live in the literature against Lewis's “Joy” version. The need formulation relies on universal seeking and on the effects of privation, not on a special feeling. |
