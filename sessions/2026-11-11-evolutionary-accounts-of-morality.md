## Author input

The author defines the target: morality as what is right and wrong *ultimately*. He states that he does not in general accept macroevolution, is unsure whether the macro/micro distinction matters here, and offers:

1. **Restraint:** if evolution built survival instincts, it is paradoxical that morality so often stops them; a limiter seems unnecessary because instincts have natural physical limits (hunger and sex are satiable).
2. **Long horizon:** moral traits concerning more than the immediate generation cannot be due to natural selection.
3. **Novel systems:** some complex systems work better under certain ethical rules; free markets work better than socialism although we did not evolve in markets, and principles of individual liberty work there too.
4. **Tribal versus cosmopolitan:** an evolved morality would be fitted to the original community and should break in an international world.
5. **To revisit:** if morality is evolutionary it may change (killing could become right); and why would slavery or rape then not be moral?

The author also believes the first argument is not found in what he has read.

## Starting position

C168–C170 prefer an objective regulative good approximated fallibly, in an objective-anchor hybrid whose constructed layer has a finite, pressure-driven genesis (C166, provisional). The project therefore already grants a partial natural genesis of norms and already denies that genesis exhausts their ground (C164, C166). The session tests whether evolutionary accounts threaten the objective layer.

## Definitions

| Term | Definition used in this session |
| --- | --- |
| $E_1$ genealogical thesis | The capacities and emotions used in moral judgment arose through biological (and cultural) evolution. A claim about the origin of a faculty. |
| $E_2$ content thesis | The content of actual moral beliefs is largely explained by selection pressures. A claim about why we believe what we believe. |
| $E_3$ constitutive thesis | What is right or wrong *is* what was selected, or what promotes fitness. A claim about moral truth. |
| $E_4$ debunking thesis | Because $E_1$/$E_2$ hold, there is no objective moral truth, or no justified belief about it. |
| Case A | Common descent granted for the sake of argument. |
| Case B | Only within-kind (micro) evolution granted. |

## Arguments and alternatives

### Which thesis the author's definition opposes

“Right and wrong ultimately” is a claim about truth. It conflicts with $E_3$ and $E_4$. $E_1$ and $E_2$ concern the origin of beliefs and faculties; by themselves they neither establish nor refute an objective standard, since origin does not settle validity in either direction. Most of the author's arguments are effective against $E_3$ and leave $E_1$ untouched, which is acceptable because $E_1$ is not the threat.

### Macroevolution and microevolution

- **Case A:** $E_1$ is available; every argument below must work while conceding it. This is the dialectically strong setting.
- **Case B:** the strong form of $E_1$ (a moral faculty inherited by descent from pre-moral ancestors) is unavailable, but within-human selection and cultural selection remain, so $E_2$ and a culturally formulated $E_4$ survive. Rejecting macroevolution therefore does not remove the challenge.

No argument retained below depends on Case B. The author's non-acceptance of macroevolution is recorded as an authorial position, not a project premise. The assistant states its own assessment to the author: common descent is very well supported and is the scientific consensus; under C183–C185 the project should not rest an ontological argument on denying it.

### 1. Restraint

Reconstruction: (P1) evolution produced the instincts; (P2) morality often restrains them; (P3) an evolved morality would exist to limit instinct; (P4) satiety already limits instinct, so the limiter is redundant; therefore an evolved morality is unexplained.

Assessment: P4 fails. Satiety limits how much one agent consumes, not whose food, partner or life is taken; conflicts of interest between individuals have no satiety bound, and aggression, status and hoarding have none either. P3 also misdescribes the rival: the standard account treats moral dispositions as *competing social instincts* (kin favour, reciprocity, reputation), so restraint is one instinct overriding another, and self-control pays in repeated interaction. As stated, the argument does not establish a non-evolutionary origin.

Two residues survive and are stronger than the original:

- **Arbitration.** When two impulses conflict, the judgment that the weaker one *ought* to prevail is not itself the stronger impulse. Evolution can explain the presence of both and which usually wins; it does not thereby supply the reason one should win. This bears on $E_3$, not $E_1$. The naturalist reply (the sense of “ought” is itself an adaptive commitment device) leads to $E_4$, not back to $E_3$.
- **Ranking of maladaptive excellence.** Reflective morality ranks highest some acts with no plausible fitness return: aid to distant strangers, sparing an enemy, care for the dying, vowed renunciation. The by-product reply calls these misfirings of small-group mechanisms; yet on reflection we endorse them more, not less. This connects to the adequacy condition C164 and to C194.

### 2. Long horizon

Selection has no foresight, but inclusive fitness covers descendants, and general planning capacity is individually adaptive. Concern for unrelated subjects centuries ahead has no plausible selective differential. The naturalist reply is by-product: general reason plus sympathy. That reply concedes that this content was not selected and assigns it to reasoning. The argument therefore succeeds against $E_2$/$E_3$ for this content and converts into the *autonomy of moral reasoning*, which the realist uses against $E_4$. It does not by itself show a non-natural source. It fits C177, C186 and C192.

### 3. Tribal versus cosmopolitan

The premise needs correction: an evolved psychology predicts exactly the breakdown we observe (parochialism, in-group bias). The force lies elsewhere: we judge parochialism to be an *error* rather than a different adaptation, and the widening of concern to be *progress* rather than drift. Evolution explains our actual morality including its failures; it does not supply the criterion by which they count as failures. This is direct support for C168–C169, where error and correction are intelligible only against a standard not constituted by practice. Live replies: by-product reasoning, cultural selection, and inclusivity as a condition-dependent plastic response.

### 4. Novel systems and markets

Not adopted as load-bearing, for three reasons shown to the author:

1. **Rival explanation.** If rules that make large orders function spread because groups adopting them outgrow others, “works better” is what cultural selection predicts. The observation does not discriminate between an objective standard and cultural selection.
2. **Missing bridge.** Functional success is not rightness. A moral criterion of “working” (prosperity for whom, at whose cost) is needed, and that criterion is the thing in question.
3. **Tension with argument 5.** The slavery argument says a practice can succeed and be wrong. The market argument, read as “it works, therefore it reflects moral truth”, uses the opposite inference. Both cannot be premises of one case.

The comparative premise about markets and socialism is also a contested empirical and political claim in its general form; C180 keeps institutional ethics optional. A neutral core is retained as an open test: whether respect for sourcehood and truthful relation (the $S$ and $R$ anchors) generalizes to environments in which no disposition was selected, and whether that generalization is better explained by an objective standard than by cultural selection. The discriminating cases are effective but wrong practices.

### 5. Mutability; slavery and rape

- **Mutability.** Distinguish change in beliefs or practices (expected under $E_2$) from change in truth (entailed only by $E_3$). The argument: under $E_3$ a different history would make killing right; it would not be right; so not $E_3$. It is valid and Moorean: its force rests on the firmness of the second premise, which a consistent error theorist rejects and a quasi-realist reinterprets. Project nuance from C189: fit is relative to bearer profile, so creatures with different natures may have different applications of the same anchors without relativism about the anchors.
- **Adaptive atrocities.** If right is what was adaptive, practices that plausibly raised reproductive or group success would have been right; they were not; so not $E_3$. Decisive against $E_3$, a position with almost no contemporary defenders. It does not touch $E_1$/$E_2$: evolution can explain both the tendencies and their condemnation, and the long acceptance of slavery fits $E_2$. The explanatory anomaly is abolition, which merges with argument 3.

### Further arguments proposed by the assistant

- **Symmetry of the genetic point.** Origin does not settle validity. This blocks simple forms of $E_4$ and equally blocks refuting evolutionary accounts by origin alone.
- **Parity.** If selection for fitness rather than truth undermines moral belief, the same reasoning reaches mathematical, modal and epistemic-normative belief, including the norms the debunker relies on. This is the moral analogue of the project's existing cognitive-trust motivation (C732); as there, it is comparative pressure and not a proof.
- **Anchor access.** The badness of agony for its subject is present in experience (anchor $P$), not inferred only from an evolved intuition. Selection explains why aversive states exist and track damage; it does not make their badness a projection. This gives the hybrid a third-factor shape: the same facts are fitness-relevant and value-constituting. The standing objection is that this presupposes a substantive evaluative truth.
- **Underdetermination.** Selection favours having norms and tolerates many stable norm systems; it does not select which is correct.
- **Scope limit.** Success of these arguments yields objectivity (C168–C170), not a divine ground. Secular realists accept all of them. C195 is unchanged.

### Consolidated candidate

Evolution, biological and cultural, explains what we in fact feel and do, including moral failure. It does not supply the criterion by which we rank evolved impulses, call some adaptive conduct atrocious and some maladaptive conduct excellent, and count widening concern as progress. The remaining fork is an objective standard or an error theory; parity and anchor access bear against the second.
