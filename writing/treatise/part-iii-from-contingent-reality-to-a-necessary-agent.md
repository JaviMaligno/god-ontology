## 7. Contingency, Brute Facts, and Regress

Part I argued that a scientific description, however complete, does not answer the question why the fundamental structure it describes obtains. Laws, constants, and boundary conditions can be stated with great precision and still leave untouched the question whether they had to be as they are, or whether anything had to be at all. This Part begins where that argument stops, with the world as something that exists but, so far as we can tell, need not have.

Call something **contingent** when it obtains but could have failed to obtain, and **necessary** when it could not have failed. The world of physics, as physics presents it, is contingent on both counts that matter: its constants and laws could apparently have been otherwise, and its existence is not something physics derives from anything more basic. Whether this appearance is correct is the subject of the next chapter. Here we ask what follows if it is.

Three stopping points offer themselves. The first is **brute contingency**: the world exists, could have not existed, and there is nothing to be said about why it does. The second is **regress**: each contingent thing is explained by a prior contingent thing, without end, and the totality is explained by nothing beyond its members. The third is a **necessary ground**: something that could not have failed to exist, on which the contingent order depends.

It would be convenient to dispose of the first two with a principle. The classical instrument is a principle of sufficient reason: every contingent fact has an explanation that entails it. The project declines the instrument, for a reason that will matter throughout. A strong principle of sufficient reason, combined with a necessary explainer, makes the explained facts necessary in turn, and so abolishes the contingency it set out to explain [C404]. What the project uses instead is weaker and more honest about its status. It is a **weak explanatory preference**: other things being roughly equal, prefer the account that leaves fewer, or less fundamental, contingent facts unexplained [C403]. This is a comparative preference among theories, not a claim that an explanation must exist. It is the same preference that keeps a scientist asking after a successful description, and it is defeasible by coherence and cost.

Under that preference, brute contingency is the lowest-ranked stopping point on the explanatory dimension. It leaves the largest and most fundamental fact — that anything contingent obtains, and this rather than that — without any account. It is not thereby refuted. The author's own formulation is the right one: if the world simply exists and nothing more can be said, that would have to be accepted; but it is more reasonable, and more continuous with the curiosity that produces science, to ask what could lie beyond, and to accept brute existence only if the alternatives fail [C403]. Regress fares no better: it relocates the unexplained totality without diminishing it.

The necessary ground is the stopping point that does not raise the same question about itself. What could not have failed to exist has no unrealized alternative with which to contrast it; asking why it exists rather than not is asking for a contrast that has no other side. This is the recognized sense in which necessary existence terminates explanation without leaving a brute fact of the same kind. The project therefore adopts, as its working hypothesis, that the contingent order has a necessary ground [C04]. The status of the adoption is preferential and abductive: it is the ranking the weak explanatory preference produces, and it stands or falls with that preference and with the failure of the alternatives, not with a demonstration.

## 8. Could Physical or Mathematical Reality Be Necessary?

The obvious way to avoid a ground beyond the physical is to make the physical itself the necessary thing. Perhaps the laws, or the mathematical structure they instantiate, could not have been otherwise, and the contingency we perceive is a limit of our perspective. This chapter shows that the option divides into cases, each of which pays a price the project is unwilling to pay.

Begin with a lemma that is neutral between every candidate ground. Suppose something necessary, $N$, grounds the contingent order. In any normal modal logic, if $N$ exists necessarily and $N$'s existence together with its necessary features entails some fact, that fact is necessary. Hence no necessary being can be a *necessitating* sufficient ground of a genuinely contingent fact. Whatever necessary thing grounds a contingent order must produce it non-necessitatingly [C404].

Apply the lemma to a necessary physical or mathematical structure. Either it necessitates its output, or it does not. If it does, nothing is contingent: not the constants, not the initial conditions, not any event, not any decision. This is **necessitarianism**, and it is coherent. The project excludes it as a working option rather than refuting it, because it denies the datum from which the inquiry began and, more seriously, it denies that any agent could have done otherwise, which the account of freedom in Part IV requires [C407].

If the necessary structure does not necessitate its output, then which output obtains needs a separate account, and there are exactly three: **chance**, a **selection law**, or **plenitude**. Chance leaves the contrast — this world rather than that — with no explanation at all; the structure is necessary, and the selection is brute. A selection law is itself either necessary, in which case the necessitation returns and contingency collapses, or contingent, in which case the regress reopens. Plenitude says that every possibility the structure admits is realized, so there is no contrast to explain; it answers “why this rather than that” with “both,” and it inflates the ontology to the size of the possible [C405].

The four stopping points are therefore: brute contingency, necessitarianism, impersonal necessity with chance or plenitude, and a fourth that the next chapter introduces. The impersonal option is not incoherent. It is the option on which the contingent world either has no contrastive explanation or has one only by ceasing to be contingent. That is the exact cost, and it is worth stating exactly, because the popular form of the option — “the laws just are necessary” — conceals it.

One further point belongs here, though its full statement comes in Part IV. A necessary mathematical structure could not be the *material* ground of the world in any case, because the conservation principles that seem to require material continuity are theorems of the structure and govern what happens within it; they cannot govern the origination of the structure itself [C434]. The question whether matter can arise without prior matter is therefore not settled by physics, and the project answers it in Chapter 11.

## 9. Why Agency Enters

The fourth stopping point is a necessary agent: a conscious being that originates the contingent order by settling on it for reasons, without being necessitated to do so. This chapter says what that adds, what it costs, and why the project prefers it. The preference is the central abductive choice of the whole ontology, and it is recorded as a preference [C05].

Define **agent causation** as the origination of an outcome by a subject, guided by reasons, such that the subject's exercise, rather than any antecedent state together with laws, is what makes the outcome actual. Define **sourcehood** as the relation in which an agent stands to an outcome it so originates [C55–C60]. The project does not require that a free act have an antecedently sufficient contrastive reason; it rejects that requirement as the determinism under dispute in another form [C60]. What it requires is that the act be the subject's, reason-guided, and among genuine alternatives.

Now return to the lemma. A necessary agent, like any necessary ground, must produce the contingent order non-necessitatingly. The difference from the impersonal case is the kind of terminus. Impersonal non-necessitating production terminates in chance or plenitude; agentic non-necessitating production terminates in a reason-guided exercise. The agent's reasons explain why the chosen structure was eligible and attractive; the agent's settling explains why it, rather than another eligible structure, obtains. The second explanation is contrastive without being necessitating. Only the agentic stopping point supplies an explanation of that shape [C406].

Three considerations make the preference more than a taste for that shape. First, explanatory form: among the four stopping points, only the agent gives the contingent order a contrastive, reason-guided explanation while leaving it contingent. Second, unification: the account of created freedom in Part IV needs consciousness and agency as a fundamental causal kind; placing them at the ground reuses what the account needs, whereas an impersonal ground must produce irreducible agency from a base that has none. Third, freedom: necessitarianism and necessitating impersonal grounds exclude created freedom, while the agentic ground and chance-based impersonal grounds permit it. This is the author's remark that the agentic option does not give rise to determinism [C406].

The objection the project takes most seriously is that a reason-guided exercise is chance with a personal name: that “the agent settled on it” adds nothing to “it happened.” The answer is the one the account of freedom gives for every free act, and it is honest about its status. The exercise belongs to a subject; it is responsive to the subject's reasons; and it is the same causal kind that the project needs, independently, to make sense of created choice. What the agentic ground adds over chance is authorship and intelligibility, not the elimination of a primitive. Both options terminate in a non-necessitated fact. The project prefers a reality whose terminus is an author to one whose terminus is a die, and it records that preference as a preference, with the conditions under which it would be withdrawn: the incoherence of necessary agency, the demonstrated emptiness of reason-guided settling, or an impersonal model that grounds contingency and created agency at lower cost [S09, C406].

It remains to say what “agent” commits the project to at this stage, and what it does not. It commits to a conscious subject with intellect and will, able to apprehend possibilities and settle among them for reasons. It does not yet commit to goodness, to perfection, to unlimited power, or to any of the attributes that the word “God” ordinarily carries. Those must be earned separately, and the next chapter takes stock of what has been.

## 10. How Far Is the Necessary Agent from God?

“Necessary agent,” “creator,” and “God” name three stages, not three descriptions of one thing [C06]. The project's rule is to discover how far the argument reaches before comparing its result with any inherited God-concept [C21]. This chapter is the audit. It reports what has been earned within the architecture, at what evidential level, and what has not.

**Uniqueness.** There is one ultimate ground of the contingent order, and it is one necessary agent. The argument is abductive: one integrated order, existential sovereignty over everything created, economy, and the conflict that two bearers of maximal power over the same order would generate together favor a single ground [C80–C83]. The project does not claim that only one necessary entity of every category exists; necessary abstracta remain open. It does claim, at the same abductive level, that exactly one necessary *agent* exists in every world, because a second one would be actual and idle [C477]. Status: preferred.

**Non-spatiality and immateriality.** The agent is not contained in created spacetime and is not a member of the physical order; absolute non-spatiality is preferred abductively without importing divine simplicity [C84–C87, C241]. Status: preferred.

**Constitution.** The agent is an underived, power-structured subject: a thin substance that is the identity unit and bearer of its powers, assembled from no prior ingredients and borne by no substrate, whose distinguishable powers are aspects — ways of acting that cannot exist without it and do not individuate it — rather than parts [C223–C228, C438]. Minimal constitutive simplicity of this kind does not entail the identities asserted by absolute simplicity, which the project has not adopted [C227]. Status: preferred at F2.

**Knowledge.** The agent knows every truth about complete actual reality without temporal acquisition (actuality-complete omniscience), knows the modal structure of possibility, and has direct, subject-indexed acquaintance with every actual conscious subject. It does not know, prior to actuality, what merely possible free agents would choose, because there is no such truth to know [C11–C13, C284, C289]. Status: accepted for the propositional core; preferred for acquaintance.

**Power.** The agent has maximal power over every coherent created target; whether it has unrestricted power over every coherent action at all is preferred but more provisional, pending the exclusion of non-created limiters [C261–C262]. Creative plenitude — that every independently possible created specification is within its capacity — is a conditional theorem of preferred premises [C478]. Status: preferred, graded.

**Presence and availability.** The agent is ontologically present to every created thing through grounding, knowledge, and causal availability, and is personally available to every conscious subject: any subject can be addressed by it and can address it [C91, C122]. Whether it exercises that availability toward a given subject, and whether reciprocal relation obtains, are further matters that depend partly on the creature [C123–C124]. Status: preferred.

**Orientation toward the good.** Every disposition the argument has established — creating for the sake of actual others with agency of their own, a standing presumption against overriding that agency, availability for relation, truthful knowledge — is directed toward the freedom of conscious subjects and truthful relation with them [C409, C470]. This is earned within the architecture, and it is not nothing: a creator could have scripted every event, overridden freely, or withheld itself. Status: preferred, earned.

**What is not earned.** No established disposition is directed toward what created subjects *feel*: toward their felt good or against their suffering. This is the P-gap, and it is exactly where the problem of evil enters the architecture [C410, C471]. Omnibenevolence, which would require orientation toward every anchor of value, universality over all bearers and acts, and a guarantee against misfit, is further away still [C411]. Perfection is not inferred from necessity or agency [R03]. Full simplicity, immutability, and the fuller classical personhood remain open.

The result is a being that is far more than a necessary cause and less than the God of classical theism. It is one, necessary, underived, non-spatial, conscious, knowing, powerful over its creation, present to everything, available to everyone, and oriented toward the freedom and the truthful recognition of what it made. Whether it is good in the sense that includes what its creatures suffer is the question the ontology has isolated but not answered, and Part VII returns to it. The word “God” is used from here on as the name of the being so described, on the understanding that its content is what has been earned, not what the word ordinarily promises.
