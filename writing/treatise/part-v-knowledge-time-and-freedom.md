## 15. Actuality-Complete Omniscience

Part IV ended with a being that is one, necessary, underived, conscious, and the ground of a contingent order in which some created agents originate their own acts. This Part asks what such a being knows, how its knowing is related to the time its creatures live in, and whether its knowing leaves their freedom intact. The order of the three questions is deliberate. The classical difficulty about foreknowledge and freedom is usually posed with a picture of divine knowledge already in place, and most of its force comes from that picture. The project's method is to say first what knowledge the architecture actually supports, and only then to ask whether the difficulty arises for it.

**Minimal definition.** Let complete actual reality be the structured whole $\mathfrak R^*=\langle G,S,H^*,\mathbf I^*,Rel^*\rangle$: the necessary agent, the generative structure it actualizes, the complete created history, every actual intervention, and every obtaining relation of dependence among them [C47]. **Actuality-complete omniscience** (ACO) is the property of knowing every truth about $\mathfrak R^*$, without temporal acquisition or updating [C11, C23]. Four features fix the notion. It is *exhaustive*: no truth about what obtains is omitted, and a false proposition presents no gap, since its true negation is among the truths known. It is *factive*: what is known is so. It is *atemporal*: the knowing is not located at a creaturely time and does not pass through states. And it is *non-productive*: knowing that $p$ does not make $p$ true; the obtaining truth grounds its inclusion in what is known [C10]. Status: accepted for the propositional core.

The word “foreknowledge” is deliberately avoided. It presupposes that divine knowledge is located before the events known, and that presupposition is exactly what the temporal semantics of Chapter 16 declines. ACO is not knowledge *in advance* of anything. It is knowledge *of* everything that obtains, in a mode that has no advance and no delay.

**Modal and counterfactual extensions.** ACO is intentionally a minimum about actuality. The fuller account adds **modal knowledge** (MKO): knowledge of the necessary constraints, of the structures within the agent's actualizing power, of the continuations each structure admits, and of the acts each agent can originate in a situation, together with their compossibility relations [C12]. The modal domain is modeled without reifying complete possible histories. A possible history is a representation of compossible manifestations of powers and constraints, not an additional concrete block alongside the actual one [C49]. Possibility itself is grounded in constitutive profiles, power profiles, identity, dependence, and compossibility, not in the cognitive act that represents it and not in the divine creative range whose extent is under examination [C48, C344–C345]. This last clause is the repair that Part IV's modal basis required: divine representation of a possibility does not make it possible, and divine capacity does not define it [C355].

The necessary constraints divide into three. Logical and identity constraints are constitutive of any coherent model; the consequences of specified mathematical structures follow from those structures whatever their ultimate ontology; and a third class contains only necessities that follow from the agent's independently established essential features [C52]. The division matters because it lets modal knowledge be exhaustive over a domain the agent does not author, without deciding whether mathematical structures are Platonic objects, divine concepts, or structural invariants.

Two exclusions define the edge of modal knowledge. First, there are no pre-actual truths about what a libertarian agent *would* choose in circumstances it never faces. Knowledge that an agent *could* originate an act under conditions $C$ is admitted; a truth that it *would* is not, because nothing grounds it [C13, C50]. The project treats this not as a limitation of omniscience but as a consequence of a general principle: where the ontology supplies no truthmaker, there is no truth to know, and failing to know it is no failure [C54]. The principle is not a device for ignoring difficult truths; it applies only where the ontology genuinely denies a truth. Second, knowledge of merely possible agents ranges over qualitative types, powers, and relations. It does not assign individual identities to persons who never exist, because there is no identity-ground for them; an actual agent, by contrast, has de re alternatives across histories, since its identity and its powers are actual [C53, C519].

**Epistemic scope versus epistemic mode.** Propositional completeness does not settle every question about how the agent knows. Knowing every truth about a subject's states is compatible with knowing them only as a third party knows a description [C283]. The project prefers something stronger. **Actual subject acquaintance** (ASA) is direct, factive, subject-indexed access to every actual conscious subject and to the first-personal organization of its actual states: the agent grasps each state *as presented to* the subject whose state it is [C284]. Adequate acquaintance discriminates first-personal differences even where a coarse third-person description does not [C286].

Three things acquaintance does not involve. It does not make the agent identical with the subject, nor the subject a constituent of the agent, nor the agent a bearer of the numerically same phenomenal token [C287–C288]. Exact discrimination, vicarious phenomenal representation, and numerical sharing of a state are three distinct models; minimal acquaintance requires only the first, and the project leaves the second live while declining the third, which would bring passibility and subject-boundary burdens with it. Nor does acquaintance with a creature's act transfer its source-role: the agent knows the act as the creature's, and does not thereby originate it [C287].

Acquaintance is actuality-indexed. Modal knowledge may represent possible subject-types and possible experiences; concrete acquaintance begins, in the order of grounding, with the actual subject and the actual state, although the agent's relation to a whole actual life is atemporal rather than acquired as the life unfolds [C285]. The embodied subject of Part IV supplies the index: what is known first-personally is the state of a persisting bearer located in created time [C312, C314].

**The role-structured profile.** Divine knowledge is therefore represented as a tuple with three roles, not three faculties: $K_G^{total}=\langle ACO_P,MKO,ASA\rangle$. The first carries complete propositional scope over what obtains; the second, modal structure; the third, direct access to actual subjectivity [C289]. They are neither temporal stages nor separable constituents. One further distinction closes the chapter. Acquaintance is unilateral: the agent has it toward every actual subject whether or not the subject has any awareness of it. *Reciprocal second-personal presence* is stronger, and requires some actual creaturely awareness, address, attention, or response [C290]. Acquaintance therefore strengthens the epistemic basis of the universal personal availability established in Part IV without entailing that every subject is in fact addressed, answered, or in communion [C291]. What the chapter earns is an omniscience that is complete about the actual, exhaustive about the possible, direct toward every subject, and silent, for principled reasons, about what free agents would have done.

## 16. Agent-Causal Eternalism and Three Kinds of Indexing

**The complete actual history.** The project's preferred temporal architecture is **agent-causal eternalism**, written E+ [C22]. One complete created history $H^*$ exists tenselessly. The agent knows it as a whole and does not update. A creature's act is located at its time; relative to the creature at an earlier time, it has not yet occurred. The tenseless proposition that the creature acts so at that time is nevertheless true in $H^*$. Alternative acts remain metaphysically available in virtue of the creature's agent-causal power, even though only one is actual. The creature's act is the truthmaker of the proposition and hence the epistemic ground of the agent's knowledge, not its effect. Interventions, if any, are constituents of the history, not revisions of a history already complete. The plus sign marks the additional commitments to libertarian agent causation, asymmetric co-production, and actuality-complete knowledge; it does not claim a new school of philosophy.

The history is *jointly but asymmetrically constituted* [C09]. The agent actualizes the structure $S$: laws, constants, boundary conditions, and kinds. Ordinary causes produce events within it. Created agents originate some of its events. Divine interventions, if there are any, are further typed contributions. The whole is grounded by all of these together, and none of the contributors is the others' equal: the agent grounds the existence of the arena and of every created power, while the creature's contribution is underived at the level of the act. The explanatory order runs from structural actualization through ordinary causes and agent origins to the history, from the history to its truths, and from its truths to their being known [S03]. Nothing in that order is a temporal sequence.

**Four kinds of openness.** The word “open” conceals four claims that E+ treats differently [C25]. The future is *epistemically* open when a creature does not know what it will do; E+ accepts this, and it is insufficient for freedom. It is *causally* open when the prior creaturely state together with the laws does not sufficiently cause the act; E+ prefers this. It is *modally* open when more than one continuation sharing the relevant causal past is genuinely possible; E+ prefers this too, with the grounding of the alternatives supplied by the power-based modality of Chapter 15. It is *alethically* open when the proposition about the future act has no determinate truth value before the act. E+ does not require this, and this is where it parts from the open-future family. The internal/external distinction between the creature's time and the agent's atemporal knowledge blocks one inference: being tenselessly true in the complete history does not entail being causally determined by the temporal past [S02]. It does not, by itself, establish freedom. Causal underdetermination and agent causation carry the positive account, and they were argued in Part IV.

Four predicates must also be kept apart: temporal location, tenseless actuality in the complete history, atemporal divine knowledge, and atemporal mode of existence [C313]. An embodied creature's conscious episode is temporally located and tenselessly actual and atemporally known, and it does *not* thereby have an atemporal mode of existence. The episode happens when it happens, inside created time, to a bearer that lives through it [C312]. Eternalism about the history is not the thesis that created experience is timeless.

**Three kinds of indexing.** Ambiguity in the word “indexed” produced most of the original conversation's difficulty, and separating three senses removed it [S01]. *Temporal* indexing locates an event at a time: the choice is made at $t_1$, not at $t_0$. *Perspectival* indexing relativizes to a creature at a time: at $t_0$, the choice has not occurred and is not epistemically available to the one who will make it. *Actuality* indexing relates a truth to the complete actual history: the agent knows the choice because it is part of $H^*$. Divine knowledge requires only the third [C23]. It may use the first two to explain creaturely talk of “not yet,” and it does so without the stronger thesis that future contingents lack truth values. “Not true yet,” said by the creature, is a perspectival remark about availability, not an alethic remark about the proposition.

Two further consequences follow. Possible histories represent modal alternatives, not simultaneously actual branches; the project's alternatives are ways the history could have gone, one of which it went [C49]. And an intervention at a time belongs to the complete history; talk of the agent “changing” the history is creature-relative shorthand for a contribution that the history contains [C22]. One empirical bridge is worth stating here because it is where this chapter touches physics: an interpretation of quantum theory on which every alternative is realized in some branch at the very events that realize a creature's settling is incompatible with libertarian settling, since settling actualizes one originable alternative; branching elsewhere is not excluded [C538]. That is a constraint on interpretation, not a physical claim.

**Open-future semantics as a controlled alternative.** The project keeps one comparison model, written F [S01]. On F, before the act no future branch is privileged and the future contingent lacks a determinate truth value; the creature's act supplies the truthmaker; an atemporal agent knows the whole indexed family of frames and the truths relative to each frame without learning sequentially. F preserves the literal, alethic reading of “not true yet.” Its cost is that truth must be made frame-relative and that the complete history can no longer be a single tenseless whole reused without explanation. E+ pays a different price: it has to answer the fixity problem directly, since it cannot say that there was no future truth to know. The project prefers E+ because it fits the author's intended picture of a complete universe with all its times, keeps the semantics of ACO simple, and does not need to make truth itself perspectival. F is retained as a diagnostic: it would be reinstated only if E+ failed the fixity test, or if the author decided that future contingents literally lack truth values. Neither has happened. Status of E+: preferred.

## 17. The Fixity Problem

Chapter 16 makes the burden explicit. If every truth about the history is tenselessly true and infallibly known, how can any act in it be one that its agent could have done otherwise? This is the modal or fixity problem, and it is the one that E+ cannot avoid by denying future truth [S01]. The chapter states the argument at its strongest and shows where it fails in this architecture. It also states, with some care, what the failure does not prove.

**Five notions.** The argument works by sliding among five things that must be kept apart: the *truth* of a proposition about an act; *knowledge* of it; the *necessity* of the act; its *causal determination* by prior events and laws; and the *freedom* of the agent who performs it. Tenseless truth is not necessity: that the history contains the act does not entail that it could not have contained another [C26]. Knowledge is not causation: the agent's knowing tracks what obtains and is not the efficient cause of what it tracks [C10]. Causal determination is not established by either truth or knowledge; it is a claim about the creaturely past and the laws, and Part IV argued that it fails for some acts. Freedom, finally, is a positive notion, sourcehood together with real alternatives [C57–C60], and it is not delivered by the mere absence of the other four.

**The argument.** In its classical form: (1) the agent knew, at every earlier time, that Alice would do $A$ at $t_1$; (2) what the agent knew at earlier times is now fixed and beyond anyone's power to alter; (3) infallible knowledge that Alice does $A$ entails that Alice does $A$; therefore (4) it is now beyond Alice's power to do otherwise than $A$. Each premise draws on a feature of divine knowledge that ACO does not have.

Premise (1) locates the knowledge at earlier creaturely times. ACO is not located at any creaturely time; it is actuality-indexed, not temporally indexed [C23, C14]. There is no past divine belief to be fixed, because there is no divine belief in the creature's past. The accidental-necessity form of the argument, which turns on the fixity of the past, has no object here. Someone who insists that the knowledge must be located before the act is asking for a temporal knower, and a temporal knower is what the project has declined; if that demand were granted, the natural response would be F, not compatibilism.

Premise (2) treats the agent's knowledge as fixed independently of what it is knowledge of. This is where the project's **actual-marker semantics** does its work [C27–C29]. Represent the modal situation as a pointed structure $\mathfrak M=\langle\mathcal H(S),R,\alpha\rangle$: the histories admitted by the structure, their accessibility relations, and the history $\alpha$ marked as actual. Total divine knowledge includes, together, the modal facts of the unpointed structure and the actual facts indexed by $\alpha$. In evaluating whether Alice could have done otherwise, the relevant causal past, capacities, and circumstances are held fixed while the history bearing the actual marker differs [C27]. In one evaluation $\alpha=h_A$ and the agent knows $A$ as actual; in another $\alpha=h_B$ and the agent knows $B$ as actual. The two are not stages through which reality or divine cognition passes; they are alternative pointed evaluations of one fixed structure. Across them the *capacity* for complete knowledge is invariant, and the *content* covaries with the actual history [C28]. The fixity argument holds the content $K_G(A)$ fixed while varying $A$; but the content is part of the very actual outcome under variation, and so cannot be held fixed. The argument's second premise is false of this knowledge [C10].

The actual marker deserves its own sentence, because a natural misreading would reinstate the problem. The marker is not a further entity, event, or divine choice added to a completed history. It designates the history that obtains; “actual” means “obtains,” and nothing more [C29]. If the marker were a divine selection among complete histories, then the agent would after all be settling Alice's act, and no semantics would save her freedom. The project's asymmetric co-production is exactly the denial of that picture: the agent actualizes the structure, and Alice's act is among the things that make one history rather than another the one that obtains [C09, C30].

Premise (3) is true and harmless. Infallibility gives the necessity of the consequence, that necessarily, if the agent knows $A$ then $A$; it does not give the necessity of the consequent, that $A$ is necessary. Atemporality adds nothing to this: that the agent knows atemporally does not make the actual index, or any actual truth, metaphysically necessary [C26]. The slide from the first to the second is the oldest error in the debate, and it is worth naming because the project's contribution is not to have noticed it but to have built a semantics in which the temptation does not arise.

**What the solution does not do.** It does not establish that Alice is free. It removes an obstacle: the coexistence of infallible atemporal knowledge with genuine alternatives is semantically consistent [S02]. Freedom itself rests on two things argued elsewhere. One is agent causation and sourcehood, which Part IV took as a fundamental causal kind and which the necessary agent itself instantiates [C05, C57–C60]. The other is the reality of alternatives, modeled as the set $Alt(A,t,h)$ of admitted histories that share the relevant causal past before $t$ and differ in the agent's act at $t$ [S02]. The freedom condition requires that the agent originate the actual act, that the alternative set be non-empty, and that no sufficient prior event-cause settle the act. Whether that condition is met by actual human beings is partly an empirical question, to be approached through the bridge premises of Part IV, not a semantic one.

Two consequences of the solution are sometimes mistaken for costs. First, there are no truths about what Alice would have done in circumstances she never faced, and so the agent does not know them [C13]. This is not a gap in omniscience; it is the absence of a truthmaker [C54]. It also has a corollary that surprised the author: two qualitatively identical agents in identical circumstances need not act alike, because they share propensities and not acts, and there is no fact, prior to the acting, about what either would do [C513]. Second, providence cannot proceed by consulting what creatures would do under hypothetical structures, since there is nothing to consult. What providence can do is the subject of Part VI; what it cannot do is a direct consequence of the present chapter.

## 18. Atemporal Priority and Temporal Experience

The last chapter of the Part asks a question that the preceding ones repeatedly touched and set aside: whether any sequence exists outside creaturely time. The classical picture has God knowing, then deciding, then creating, then observing; the project's picture has none of these as stages. The chapter says what replaces them, and then turns to the creature's side, where sequence is real.

**No sequence in the agent.** The agent is atemporal with respect to creaturely time [C14]. The only orders the project recognizes are the creature's temporal order and the order of grounding, and the latter is not a succession. Structural actualization grounds the history together with ordinary causes, agent origins, and divine contributions; the history grounds its truths; the truths ground their being known [S03]. Read as a sequence of divine states, this would be a temporal God after all. Read as it is intended, it says which facts depend on which, and it says that no divine knowledge of a free choice as actual is metaphysically prior to the history containing that choice [C30]. The agent does not first know and then permit, first decide and then observe. Whatever is contingent in the agent, its intentional and acquaintance relations to what it made, has contingent content; the basic conscious capacity that bears those relations is underived and atemporal [C311].

The same replacement applies to deliberation. For created agents, settling is a temporal episode with preparation, apprehension, and execution. For the agent, the project substitutes an atemporal field of comprehended possibilities and reasons; the common functions are subject unity, apprehension, reason-guidance, and efficacious settling, and everything temporal in the created case belongs only to some created implementations [S06]. That the agent settles among eligible structures for reasons, as Part IV argued, does not mean that it weighs and then chooses. It means that its settling is reason-guided; the “then” is ours.

Two metaphors are retained under discipline. “Observation” is acceptable for exhaustive epistemic access to what obtains; it must not suggest sensory reception, waiting, or the acquisition of information [S03]. “Response” is acceptable for a divine contribution that is grounded partly in a creature's act; it must not suggest that the agent is later than the act it answers. An intervention responsive to a prayer is a constituent of the history whose grounds include the prayer, not a reaction that follows it in the agent's time [C22]. Part VI develops the typing of such contributions; here the point is only that responsiveness and succession come apart.

**Sequence in the creature.** Nothing in E+ removes time from the creature. Its conscious episodes are located in created time, occur to a persisting bearer, and are lived through in order [C312, C314–C315]. Eternalism says that every such episode tenselessly exists in the complete history and is atemporally known; it does not say that the creature's experience of succession is illusory, and the project has not adopted any thesis about whether the felt present is an irreducible feature of embodied consciousness or a feature of its representation. That question belongs to the realization of consciousness and is not needed by the argument. What E+ denies is only that the future is alethically open, and Chapter 16 showed that this denial leaves epistemic, causal, and modal openness where the creature lives them.

Acquaintance is where the two sides meet. The agent grasps each episode as presented to the subject at its time, without itself being at that time [C284–C285]. The subject's “now” is part of what is grasped; it is not shared, because the agent has no now to share, and it is not lost, because the first-personal organization of the state, including its temporal perspective, is exactly what acquaintance discriminates [C286]. This is also why the creature's temporal experience is not diminished by being known: to be known as one lives is not to be lived by another.

One consequence reaches beyond the creature's life. Under E+ and the simple persistence of subjects argued in Part IV, a temporal gap in a bearer's existence is a disconnected location of one object within complete actual reality, not a ceasing followed by a fresh start; the intuition that a gap is fatal to identity is a presentist intuition, and E+ has already declined presentism [C508]. The trunk states the conditions under which one individual may have such a disconnected career: the identity fact itself, the same origin anchor, essence compatibility, non-branching, and one grounded career [C447–C450]. Part VII returns to what this makes possible. Here it is recorded because it is the point at which the temporal semantics of this Part, adopted for the sake of knowledge and freedom, turns out to bear on a question it was not built to answer.

**What the Part has earned.** An omniscience that is complete about the actual, exhaustive about the possible, and directly acquainted with every subject; a temporal architecture in which the history is one tenseless whole to which creatures contribute underived acts; a semantics on which infallible atemporal knowledge and genuine alternatives are consistent; and an agent in whom there is no sequence, related to creatures for whom there is. What it has not earned is freedom itself, which rests on the agency of Part IV and on bridge premises still open; and it has said nothing yet about what the agent does within the history it grounds. Part VI begins there.
