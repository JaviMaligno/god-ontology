## 19. Creation as Intervention Zero

Part V left the agent related to its creatures without succession: grounding the history, knowing it, acquainted with every subject in it, and never later than anything it answers. This Part asks what the agent *does* within the history it grounds. The question has a classical shape, creation, conservation, miracle, providence, and the project keeps the shape while changing the joints. The first change is to treat creation itself as the limiting case of intervention, so that every later divine contribution can be described with the same vocabulary and its difference from creation stated exactly.

**The form of a divine contribution.** An intervention is a divine-originating causal contribution that is a constituent of the complete history in which it occurs [C15, C22]. It is written as a tuple $I_j=\langle G,\tau,x,m,e_t\rangle$: the agent, a target type, a particular target, a mode of contribution, and a temporal manifestation [S04]. In the eternalist semantics of Part V, an intervention does not edit a history that was already actual. It changes which complete history bears the actual marker in the relevant comparison: with the shared prefix $P_t$, the history with the intervention is $P_t\oplus I_t\oplus C_I$ and the history without it is $P_t\oplus C_0$, and these are different histories, one of which obtains. What is excluded is only a meta-temporal process in which the agent first possesses one actual history and later revises it [C22, S04]. Law- and boundary-level contributions may change the space of admissible histories as well as its actual member; none of these differences is a change in divine knowledge.

**Creation as the zero case.** Creation, written $I_0$, is the actualization of existence and of the generative structure $S$ itself: laws, constants, boundary conditions, kinds, and powers [C15, C97–C100]. It is retained in the intervention vocabulary as *intervention zero*, following the author's original formulation, with one honest qualification: strictly it constitutes the causal arena rather than intervening within an antecedent one [S04]. The qualification matters for two later claims. The regularity of the arena, which Chapter 21 will need, is something creation establishes and later interventions presuppose; and the general dependence of everything on $I_0$ must be excluded from the definition of miracle, or every event would be one.

**Root creation and its range.** Part IV established that the agent produces the created order without material input, by an originative existential power exercised as a contingent, specification-bearing act [C223–C238, C487–C491]. The trunk has since fixed how far that power reaches. A complete created specification describes a *root created basis*: a generative basis with no existentially prior created producer [C369]. Under conditional modal ultimity, well-founded existential dependence, and the modal uniqueness of the underived ground, the agent directly originates every possible root basis [C370], and does so agentically, as a selected exercise of its essential power rather than by emanation [C371]. The resulting range is *qualified creative plenitude*: every essence-compatible admissible root specification is within the agent's creative capacity [C372, C375]. The qualification is exact and deserves its own sentence. Eligibility excludes only descriptions that are incoherent about the acting subject, such as a world that requires its ground to be spatially contained in it; these are not tasks the agent fails at, any more than a square circle is, and the filter may use only independently established essential features, never a stipulated incapacity [C263, C372]. The result is preferred at F2 and inherits the abductive status of the modal-ultimity premise on which it rests [C376]. It does not by itself deliver control over what has already been created, nor access to every operation after a fixed past, nor unrestricted omnipotence [R124].

**What creation is not.** Two inferences are blocked here because the rest of the Part depends on their being blocked. Creation does not entail that the agent produces every later effect: existential grounding, proximate production, the capacity to substitute for a created cause, and actual exercise are four relations, not one [C379]. And the power to originate does not, by itself, include a power to end; whether the agent can annihilate is a separate question with a separate answer [C389]. Both are taken up in Chapter 21.

## 20. A Taxonomy of Intervention

**Targets.** Interventions are typed first by what they directly target [S04, C35–C46]. Boundary interventions ($I_B$) configure initial or boundary conditions. Law-level interventions ($I_L$) alter a law or introduce a local nomic exception. State interventions ($I_X$) change a physical state without thereby settling any choice. Circumstantial interventions ($I_C$) supply information, evidence, salience, reasons, incentives, opportunities, or constraints; they subdivide by which element of the agent's deliberative situation they touch. Power interventions ($I_P$) alter an agent's cognitive, volitional, or bodily capacities, and may restore, enlarge, restrict, or remove them. Act interventions ($I_A$) target the originating act itself, and remove the agent's authorship of that act if the agent's contribution is replaced.

**Two independent axes.** The target of an intervention and its relevance to agency are independent [S04]. A boundary, law, or state intervention has an impersonal direct target and may later affect agents indirectly; it may instead affect no agent's situation at all. The project permits agent-neutral intervention without assigning it a purpose, because divine action is not defined by anthropocentric aim; reasons for such action belong to providence and goodness, not to the taxonomy [S04]. Conversely, the physical magnitude, visibility, or frequency of an intervention does not measure its effect on freedom. A large environmental change can leave sourcehood untouched; a small alteration of a decision mechanism can override it [C115–C116, S19].

**The autonomy test.** Represent an agent's deliberative situation as a tuple of available information, reasons and incentives, feasible opportunities, and agentive capacities; an intervention transforms it [S04]. Two sets are then distinguished: the acts *physically executable* after the intervention, and the acts that *remain originable* by the agent after it [C36]. Freedom for a token act, with the intervention already among the circumstances, requires that the agent originate the act, that more than one act remain originable, and that the intervention not be a sufficient cause of the act [C36]. Two ways of removing token autonomy are thereby separated: *option closure*, which reduces executable alternatives to one, and *source override*, which sufficiently settles the act while other acts remain executable [C39–C40]. A circumstantial intervention that influences without sufficing leaves the act the agent's: the intervention and the response are distinct contributions to the one history [C41]. This is the model's account of divine address, which Part IV introduced as an exercise of personal availability and which is here typed as circumstantial contribution or bare regard, never as override [C466].

**The diachronic level.** The token test is necessary and not sufficient for the agency the creation reason concerns. The author's development of the point distinguishes token sourcehood from authorship of the agent's governing standpoint across a life, and represents the diachronic *agency ecology* as a non-scalar profile: token sourcehood, usable originable range, relevant capacities, sufficient epistemic access, revisability, self-formative contribution, and viable routes of resistance or correction [C297, C299]. No creature authors its initial constitution, so robust created agency cannot require global self-creation; it requires that, once capacities develop, some of the agent's acts make a non-redundant difference to its later governing states [C298, C300]. Five levels of control are then kept apart: influence, steering, governance capture, option closure, and source override [C302]. *Governance capture* is the cumulative case: adaptive control of high-leverage developmental inputs that counteracts the agent's locally free deviations and restricts its routes of revision, without determining any token act [C301]. Three standings follow, and the distinction among them is what dissolves the apparent fork between possible revision and actual self-formation: token freedom for one settlement; *robust capacity*, which requires an actual, practically accessible, uncaptured route of revision or resistance; and *realized diachronic authorship*, which is a historical achievement requiring actual self-formative contributions [C305–C307]. A newly created mature agent can have the first two without the third; a later loss of capacity does not erase authorship already realized.

The question-driven track added two refinements that the trunk model does not state and does not exclude [C541]. A *funnel* is a curator policy under which every originable sequence of the agent's choices lands in a target set of life profiles: every fork real, one destination; it closes the option range at the level of the life rather than the act, and it need not be adaptive [C497]. And *evaluative authorship* names the agent's ownership of the standards by which it settles; cumulative influence that installs those standards without passing through the agent's evaluation removes it while leaving token autonomy intact [C498]. The distinction between rational influence, which supplies reasons, information, opportunities, or recognition the agent evaluates, and a-rational influence, which shapes the evaluative apparatus itself, says which cumulative influence is compatible with authorship and which is not [C499]. Education, testimony, argument, and example are on one side; engineered appetite and installed salience on the other. Manipulation, so typed, damages the authorship of the same persisting subject; it does not produce a new one [C520].

## 21. Miracle and Secondary Causation

**Three levels.** Intervention, miracle, and providence belong to different ontological levels, and most confusion in the topic comes from running them together [C42–C45, S04]. An intervention is a token divine causal contribution. A miracle is an *event* classified by its causal explanation. Providence is a *global relation* among divine actualization, any interventions, and the whole history. The three are defined in that order, because each uses the one before.

**Secondary causation.** The default causal distribution of the actual order must be fixed before miracle can be defined, since miracle is an exception to it. The author fixes it as *grounding with sparse direct action*: global divine grounding, real secondary efficacy, and no universal immediate divine production of derivative tokens [C380]. An ordinary later effect is proximately produced by a created cause, and not by the agent; the agent grounds the existence of the cause and the causal arena of the effect, and grounding does not transfer proximate authorship [C379, S41]. This is not merely permission for secondary causes; it is the preferred claim that the agent in fact does not produce ordinary effects immediately, although it could. “Sparse” names a distribution, not a numerical rarity.

What the agent could do is stated by *source-neutral causal substitutability*: for every eligible created outcome whose identity does not constitutively require another agent to supply its contrast, the agent has the capacity to settle it directly [C382–C383, C397]. The restriction to source-neutral outcomes is where freedom is protected. The agent may be able to produce the bodily configuration that follows a free choice; if the agent supplies the settling, the result is not the same act, because the original act essentially included creaturely settlement [C384]. “Make the creature originate this freely by originating it instead” is not a difficult task but a malformed one [R127]. Capacity, moreover, entails neither actual exercise nor universal concurrence [R128].

**Constitutive control and its limit.** From substitutability, relational immanence, and the intervention taxonomy, the trunk derives *constitutive control* over what has already been created, in three ranges [C385]. Positive control ranges over eligible source-neutral changes of state and constitution [C386]. Persistence-sensitive control ranges over changes to a persisting bearer, and only where the requested result and preservation conditions are compatible with its numerical identity: an essential feature cannot be removed while the bearer is kept, and destroying a thing and producing a qualitative match is replacement, not modification [C387–C388]. Annihilative control is the range the originative power does not supply by itself [C389]; the author derives it by another route. In the eternalist semantics, a temporal entity has a complete life-profile that may have a terminal boundary, and one atemporal grounding relation can ground a bounded profile without changing or withdrawing anything [C392–C393]. Annihilation is then the direct divine settlement of a terminal boundary with no specified created remainder, not the production of a positive non-being [C394]; it may be local, within continuing created time, or total, terminating the created temporal order without any later empty state [C395]. All three ranges are preferred at F2 under their independent eligibility conditions [C401], and none implies exercise: the capacity to end is not a policy to end [C400]. What the type-level result does not establish by itself is access to each operation after an arbitrarily fixed past [C390]. The trunk supplies that separately: a history-sensitive task is typed by operation, target, outcome, created boundary, preservation profile, and source roles; its eligibility requires an admissible complete history embedding the exact prefix and the requested outcome without already containing the divine performance; and, given that $G$ retains every essential operation type across any created prefix and that independently eligible prefix and outcome conditions compose locally, every eligible fixed-prefix task has a relevant witness in which the same $G$ intentionally performs it [C406–C418]. Source-neutral physical, nomic, and constitutive interventions are thereby within history-sensitive range; in joint outcomes such as communication, $G$'s contribution is within range while the creature's uptake remains co-produced [C413–C415].

**Miracle.** With the default fixed, a miracle is an event to which some intervention other than creation directly contributes and which the standing structure together with secondary causes does not exhaustively explain [C43, S04]. Direct contribution means a non-derivative constituent of the event's sufficient production, not remote causal ancestry, so that the downstream effects of one intervention are not all miracles. Excluding creation prevents the general dependence of everything on the agent from making everything miraculous. Three things the definition does not require are worth listing because the popular concept requires all of them: violation of a law, spectacle, and scientific detectability [C44]. A direct law-level, state, informational, or power contribution may ground a miracle when the conditions hold; a remotely arranged ordinary encounter is not one, however providential.

**Response without succession.** An intervention can be responsive, in created causal order, to an actual condition, request, act, or threat, while belonging timelessly to the history [C118, S19]. The condition, the response, and their relation are together constituents of the history and objects of the agent's knowledge, without temporal learning. The model needs no rejected counterfactuals for this: the reason for an actual response can include an actual truthmaker, the condition itself, rather than a purported truth about what a merely possible agent would have done [C13, S35]. What remains an F2 problem is self-consistency, when a response alters the conditions under which the condition it answers occurs; the project records it as open and separate from cumulative control [S35].

## 22. Providence as Architecture

**The global relation.** Providence adds no causal arrow. It organizes the relation among the agent's actualization of the structure, the agent's reasons, any interventions, and the co-produced history: $Prov_A=\langle OEC(G,\mathcal C_0,S^*),\mathcal R_G,\mathbf I^*,H^*\rangle$ [C45, C117]. Membership of a creaturely act in a providential history transfers none of its authorship to the agent [C46]. This is *qualified agency-respecting providence*: the agent retains the power to intervene and even to override sourcehood, while the reason for which sourcehood was created normally counts against replacing it [C115, C119].

**Control without exhaustive pre-programming.** The classical picture has providence as a plan realized either by determining every event or by consulting what creatures would do under every circumstance. The first is excluded by the creation reason and the second by the absence of anything to consult [C13, C106]. What the project puts in their place is architecture: the agent settles the structure, within which ordinary causes and creaturely settlings produce the history, together with whatever the agent contributes directly. Physical features that follow from a reason-supported structure are thereby derivatively explained without a separate reason for each [C120]. The author's own image is exact: a carefully prepared board, not a film already shot.

**The defeasible presumption.** The agency-oriented creative reason supplies a presumption over interventions. If two interventions adequately serve the same relevant aim and one preserves more originable alternatives, capacities, or sourcehood without another known defeating cost, that one is favored, other things equal [C115, S19]. This is a reason relation, not a prohibition and not a decision rule. Intervention can enhance, restore, protect, reduce, or remove agency, and so non-intervention has no automatic priority; what would be incoherent is to treat created sourcehood as a central rationale while assigning no cost at all to its unnecessary replacement [S19]. The comparison among interventions is made without forcing its dimensions into a scalar: change in feasible options, change in originable options, change in capacity, and the status of the source.

**Why physical magnitude does not measure source control.** “Lower control,” in this presumption, concerns authorship, not the size, visibility, or rarity of the divine act [C116]. Restoring a capacity, disclosing a truth, reopening an escape route, or protecting an agent against another controller are agency-supporting interventions, whatever their scale. Determining a single choice is source override, however subtle. The presumption ranks by the second axis of Chapter 20, never the first.

**Providence over lives.** Because the agency ecology, not the token act alone, is what the creation reason concerns, the presumption extends to the diachronic level: it ranges over the agent's whole profile across a life [C304]. On the question-driven track this yields two consequences the trunk model does not state. Providence is not curation: agency-respecting providence excludes funnels and standard-installation as well as exhaustive outcome control, because curating a life into a target profile, however benevolent, would make the life the curator's with the creature's signature, and that is what the reason for creating agents rules out [C501–C502, R192]. And divine address preserves both levels of agency when it is rational influence; address engineered to funnel or to install standards would not [C503]. Both follow, on the typed account of creative reason, from the requiring sourcehood reason, whose target includes the diachronic level: the exclusion of funnels is an admissibility condition, not only a preference [C527].

One consequence of the diachronic extension runs against a natural hope. A policy of preventing grave harms by repeated selection of an agent's circumstances is itself a curator policy over the agent's field of consequences, and past some density it funnels [C504]. Lowering the threshold of preventive intervention is therefore not free, even when each intervention preserves the token act. This is one of the two policy-level reasons that Part VII will use in the treatment of permitted suffering, and it is stated here because it is a fact about providence before it is a fact about evil.

**Case checks.** The distinctions hold across the representative cases the project uses to test them [S04]. An event exhausted by the structure and secondary causes is not a miracle and is included in providence globally. A remotely arranged ordinary encounter is providential only, and may leave every act in it free. A direct physical healing or a restorative power intervention is a miracle at the directly affected event and may expand agency. A direct revelation followed by a free response is a miracle at the revelation and not at the response. A source override is a miracle at the produced event, and the token act is not free. None of these cases requires the agent to be later than anything, to learn anything, or to have consulted what anyone would have done.

**What the Part has earned.** A single vocabulary in which creation is the limiting case; a range for creation that is universal over eligible roots and honest about its abductive premise; a default causal distribution on which created causes really produce and the agent really could substitute; three ranges of control over what exists, including the power to end; a definition of miracle that needs neither violation nor spectacle; a providence that is architecture rather than script, with a presumption against unnecessary replacement of sourcehood at both the token and the diachronic level; and responsiveness without succession. What it has not earned is the history-sensitive range, which the trunk takes up next, and any judgment about whether the agent's providence is good, which Part VII must earn separately.
