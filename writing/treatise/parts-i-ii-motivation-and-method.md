# Part I — Motivation: Description, Explanation, and the Limits of Science

## 1. When Explanation Is Rich Description

The inquiry began with a sentence attributed to a mathematician: science describes but does not explain. The author agreed with it and wanted its foundations, because ordinary language blurs it. We say that things fall because of gravity, and it sounds like an explanation; but gravity is the description of how things fall, and what has been added is a name and a law under which the falling is subsumed. The sentence is not an accusation against science. It is a remark about words, and about what a very successful practice does and does not do [U01].

Three uses of “explain” must be separated. In the *ordinary* sense, to explain is to make something intelligible to someone, and a good description often does that. In the *technical* sense that philosophy of science has refined, to explain is to subsume under a law, to exhibit a mechanism, to unify diverse phenomena under a common structure, or to trace a causal history. And in the *deeper* sense the author was reaching for, to explain is to say why things are the way they are rather than otherwise. The project's claim is that the first two are forms of description at increasing depth, and that the third is what remains when the description is complete [C01].

The point is not that description is shallow. Prediction, mechanism, and unification are the achievements of science, and they are achievements of description: a prediction describes what will happen given what is; a mechanism describes how a process runs; a unification describes several things as instances of one. When the assistant, in the original conversation, offered “the behavior follows from the geometry described by general relativity” as an explanation, the author noticed that the sentence itself used the word “describe,” and that nothing had been added to description except a change of vocabulary [U02]. That observation is the seed of the project.

Why does gravity not end the regress? Because each descriptive level presupposes a structure it does not account for. Newtonian gravity described falling; general relativity described Newtonian gravity as a limit of geometry; a future theory may describe the geometry as a limit of something else. At every step the question “why this structure?” is answered by a deeper structure, and at the last step it is answered by nothing. The regress is not a defect of physics; it is the shape of description. What ends it, if anything does, is not a further description.

## 2. What Science Presupposes and What It Does Not Decide

Science presupposes, and cannot establish from within, that nature is regular, that the future will resemble the past in the respects that matter, that mathematics applies to the world, and that the world is intelligible to minds like ours. These are not hypotheses it tests; they are the conditions of its testing anything. The author's second question was about the mathematical one: is it not enough to say that mathematics arises from experience of the world and so unsurprisingly describes it? The project takes the reply seriously and records its limits. The abstractions of higher mathematics carry input from reality, as the author said; but that mathematics developed from experience does not explain why the world has a structure that mathematics captures, and “the universe is just mathematical, it is just what it is” is a stopping point that feels easy and bland, and is not thereby wrong [U03]. Part III will give that stopping point its exact cost rather than dismissing it.

The central methodological distinction of the project is between *methodological success* and *metaphysical entailment* [C183]. That a method works is evidence about the world; it is not, by itself, a metaphysical thesis about what the world is. Methodological naturalism, the practice of seeking natural causes, is not metaphysical naturalism, the thesis that there are none but natural causes, and the success of the first does not entail the second. The project uses science in three ways, each governed by this distinction: as *empirical input* to its constructions; as a *realization constraint* on what the actual world can contain; and as a *bridge-mediated defeater*, where a scientific finding defeats a metaphysical claim only when an explicit bridge premise shows that the claim entails an empirically contradicted consequence [C184]. Without the bridge, a finding is information; with it, a test. Science may inform metaphysical construction without becoming definitionally prior or a standing veto beyond its demonstrated domain [C185].

One corollary is stated here because it recurs. Current non-detection is not a metaphysical veto [R27]. That no instrument detects something is evidence about instruments and about the thing's interaction with them; it becomes evidence of non-existence only with a bridge premise saying that the thing, if it existed, would have been detected. The project applies this symmetrically: it does not infer the impossibility of disembodied consciousness from its non-observation, and it does not infer the existence of anything from the absence of a disproof.

## 3. The Question Beyond the Model

Fundamental structure invites interpretation because it is the point at which description stops describing. The laws, the constants, the boundary conditions, and the kinds of thing there are: physics states them with great precision and does not derive them. One can ask why they are as they are, and one can ask why there are any. These questions are not gaps that a better model will fill; they are questions of another kind, about the model as a whole.

The project does not pretend that asking them compels an answer. Brute facts are a live stopping point: it is coherent to say that the fundamental structure exists, could have failed to, and has no account [C435]. The author's own posture is the right one to record: if that is how things are, it would have to be accepted; but it is an odd attitude for the curious, since the whole of science consists in not being satisfied with “it is so and that is all,” and it is more consistent with that curiosity to ask what could lie beyond, accepting brute existence only if the alternatives fail [U01, C435]. That is a weak explanatory preference, not a principle of sufficient reason, and Part III says why the difference matters.

The author also recorded, early in the conversation, an unexpected lean toward naturalism when a lean toward theism had been anticipated [U04]. The project keeps that record. A construction that began from a felt pull in the other direction and reached its conclusion by argument is a different thing from a construction that began from its conclusion, and the reader is entitled to know which this is.

# Part II — Method: Constructing Rather than Importing an Ontology

## 4. Standards of Metaphysical Comparison

The project's aim is to build the strongest coherent ontology the author can endorse. Originality is welcome and secondary. Classical theism is a comparison target and a possible destination, not a package of premises [research method]. This fixes the standard of success: not demonstration, which the project does not expect at fundamental underdetermined junctions, but a reasoned working choice.

The standard has six components, and every comparative judgment in the treatise uses some of them: internal coherence and compatibility with the rest of the ontology; explanatory reach and unification; comparative ontological cost; fit with the phenomena and commitments the project takes seriously; resistance to load-bearing objections; and the author's considered intelligibility, satisfaction, and preference, identified as authorial judgment rather than disguised deduction [S09]. The last is not a concession. An ontology one cannot endorse is not one's ontology, and the honest course is to say where preference enters rather than to dress it as proof.

Every conclusion is therefore labeled by its kind of support: *deductive*, when it follows from accepted premises in a logic the project accepts; *abductive*, when it is the best available explanation by the standard above; *coherence-based*, when its support is fit with the rest; *preferential*, when the standard's last component is doing the work; and *theological* or *faith*, when the claim goes beyond what the project has established and is held as trust with stated content and attention to counterevidence [C201, C457]. A preferred position is the working architecture, not one side of a permanently reset tie; revision requires reasons strong enough to outweigh both the existing case and the downstream architecture that depends on it; and downstream modules conditional on a preferred premise stay in place while that premise is stress-tested [S09].

The project also separates three axes that one label would conflate [research method]. *Commitment status* says whether a claim is accepted, preferred, provisional, open, deferred, or rejected. *Development depth* says how far it has moved from a clear concept to an integrated, stress-tested formal account, on a scale from conceptual statement through architectural placement and local formalization to integrated stress test. *Evidential maturity* says which supports have actually been examined: authorial direction, internal argument, external provenance, and experiential or theological context, the four layers no one of which is dismissed because another cannot reach it. An accepted claim may remain conceptual; a rejected model may be highly formalized; formal detail is not confidence.

Two procedural rules complete the standard. Under the *default-acceptance* rule, the author comments selectively, and proposals not objected to in the next reply are retained at their proposed status; explicit objections override [research method]. Under the *priority discipline*, work is ordered by explicit authorial direction, then by unresolved material already present in the inherited conversation, then by prerequisites on which later attributes depend, and only then by newly opened branches; a spontaneous question is banked and is not, merely by recency, a reason to reprioritize [C77, C324].

## 5. Necessity, Possibility, and Ground

The modal vocabulary is fixed here because every later argument uses it. Something is *contingent* when it obtains but could have failed to; *necessary* when it could not have failed; *possible* when it could have obtained. *Ground* is the relation in which one thing depends for its existence or obtaining on another; it is asymmetric and well founded, and the order of grounding is not a temporal order [C29–C30].

*Conceivability and its limits.* Imaginability is defeasible evidence of possibility, never its constitution: a detailed imagining is a partial model that bears on consistency and compossibility and on nothing else, and detailed imagination provides evidence only when it is stable under scrutiny and not carried merely by assignment [C354]. Logical consistency is necessary and insufficient: a consistent set of laws may admit no complete history with the stated boundary conditions [C281].

*What possibility is.* For a created specification, possibility is constitutive admissibility [C344–C345]: the specification is well formed; satisfies logical, identity, and mathematical constraints; its kinds satisfy their real definitions; its laws and changes fit the manifestation profiles of their bearers; its components can obtain jointly in one order; and its basis has a coherent dependent profile that does not combine dependence with underived ultimity in the same respect. A partial specification is possible only if it has at least one complete admissible extension [C347]. Possible structures and possible histories are distinct: a history must satisfy an admissible structure, and its token events must be compatible with the powers and shared past the structure specifies [C348].

*Five things that possibility is not.* It is not divine representation: modal knowledge tracks admissibility and does not make it true [C355]. It is not divine capacity: a criterion of possibility that quantified over what the agent can actualize would make the claim that the agent can actualize every possibility empty [C274]. It is not selection: which admissible specification is actual is a further fact. It is not actuality: possible histories represent compossible ways reality could be and are not additional concrete universes [C349, C514]. And it is not fixed by any prior created thing: type-level constitutive profiles can constrain possible kinds without tokens of those kinds existing [C350]. What ultimately grounds uninstantiated profiles, necessary structural fact, an independently established feature of the agent, or a mixed account, is recorded as open, and the project says so rather than resolving it by stipulation [C350].

*The necessary constraints* divide into logical and identity constraints constitutive of any coherent model, the consequences of specified mathematical structures whatever their ultimate ontology, and necessities that follow from the agent's independently established essential features [C52]. The division lets modal claims be assessed without first deciding whether mathematics is a realm, a set of divine concepts, or a structural invariant.

## 6. The Adversarial and Provenance Method

*Load-bearing objections.* Pressure is applied where the model bears weight: at premises that change the ontology, at inferences whose exact scope a major result depends on, at apparent compatibilities that may conceal contradiction, at verbal formulations that may conflate relations, and at claims several later commitments depend on [research method]. Every session record ends with the strongest objections it could find and their outcomes, and every treatise chapter carries the objection it takes most seriously. The method has a formalization trigger: a claim is moved to a formal statement when a major inference depends on its exact scope, when a compatibility may hide a contradiction, when two formulations may conflate relations, when several commitments depend on it, or when a serious objection turns on truth conditions, modality, temporal order, or causal structure. Otherwise the main development stays conceptual and architectural, and lateral branches are not deepened merely because they admit detail.

*Sources.* The project reads ancient and contemporary sources as comparison and provenance, not as authority. Its precedent map classifies each element as established, synthesis, potentially distinctive, or unassessed, and records the closest checked precedent [research map]. Where a session could not verify a citation online, the record says so, and the literature pass is a standing item of remaining work. Comparisons with named positions are localized to provenance or to a load-bearing difference, so that the argument is not interrupted by a running commentary on Molinism, open theism, or classical theism [C24].

*Decisions, open questions, and cautious novelty.* Every decision is recorded with its status, reason, and provenance in a commitment ledger; rejected and corrected moves are recorded with the same care, because a project that only records what it accepts cannot show what it declined and why. Open questions are ranked and revisited. And novelty is treated as a claim that must be earned: the project calls its results syntheses of established components, records what may be project-specific, and defers any stronger claim to a comparison broader than it has yet made. A position built this way can be wrong. It cannot easily be wrong without the record showing where.
